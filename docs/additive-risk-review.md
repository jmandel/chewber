## 1. E407 — Carrageenan

### Current classification: Moderate

### Regulatory status
- **JECFA/WHO**: "Not specified" ADI (the most favorable safety rating; reaffirmed 2014). Considers food-grade (undegraded) carrageenan safe.
- **EFSA (2018 re-evaluation)**: Maintained approval. Established ADI of 75 mg/kg bw/day. Noted some uncertainty about GI effects but concluded no safety concern at current exposure levels.
- **FDA**: GRAS. Approved since 1961. Rejected 2016 citizen petition to ban it, citing insufficient evidence of harm from food-grade carrageenan.
- **National Organic Standards Board**: Removed from approved organic list in 2018 (precautionary), but USDA did not follow through; it remains permitted.

### Key evidence
- **Tobacman (2001)** and subsequent reviews raised concerns about gut inflammation. However, much of this work conflated **degraded carrageenan** (poligeenan, a known GI irritant, MW <50 kDa) with **food-grade carrageenan** (MW 200-800 kDa). EFSA's 2018 review explicitly addressed this conflation.
- **Bhattacharyya et al. (2017)**: Human RCT (12 patients with ulcerative colitis) found carrageenan-free diet reduced relapse. Very small sample, single study, not replicated.
- **McKim et al. (2019, Critical Reviews in Toxicology)**: Comprehensive review concluded food-grade carrageenan does not cause GI inflammation in humans at dietary exposure levels.
- **Weiner et al. (2014)**: Review of 45 feeding studies; no adverse GI effects from food-grade carrageenan.

### Assessment
The Tobacman work is methodologically important but doesn't translate to food-grade carrageenan at dietary levels. However, the debate is real, ongoing, and supported by at least some human evidence (Bhattacharyya). EFSA's acknowledgment of "some uncertainty" justifies not treating this as fully settled.

### Recommendation: **MODERATE** ✓ Keep
The controversy is scientifically grounded enough (even if overstated) to warrant a moderate penalty. It's the most-debated emulsifier for a reason. The Bhattacharyya human study, while small, is more than most additives have. Moderate is appropriate — not punitive, not dismissive.

## 2. E433/E435/E436 — Polysorbates 80/60/65

### Current classification: Moderate

### Regulatory status
- **JECFA**: ADI of 25 mg/kg bw/day for polysorbate 80 (established). No safety concerns raised in most recent evaluation.
- **EFSA (2015 re-evaluation)**: Maintained approval. ADI of 25 mg/kg bw/day. Exposure assessment showed intake well below ADI.
- **FDA**: GRAS. Polysorbate 80 widely used in pharmaceuticals (including injectable vaccines) at much higher doses than food use.

### Key evidence
- **Chassaing et al. (2015, Nature)**: The landmark study. Showed that polysorbate 80 and CMC at 1% w/v in drinking water disrupted gut microbiome and promoted low-grade inflammation and metabolic syndrome in mice. This is the study driving the moderate classification.
- **Critical limitations**:
  - **Dose**: 1% w/v in drinking water ≈ ~1,250 mg/kg bw/day for a mouse. The JECFA ADI for humans is 25 mg/kg bw/day. Typical human dietary exposure is estimated at 0.1-5 mg/kg bw/day. The mouse dose was **250-12,500× typical human exposure**.
  - **Species translation**: Mouse gut physiology and microbiome are substantially different from humans. The thin mouse mucus layer is particularly susceptible to emulsifier disruption.
  - **No human replication**: As of 2024, no published human RCT has demonstrated that polysorbate 80 at dietary exposure levels causes gut barrier disruption or metabolic changes.
- **Chassaing et al. (2022, Gastroenterology)**: Follow-up RCT in humans with CMC (not polysorbate 80) showed some microbiome changes, but the clinical significance was unclear and the study was in a controlled metabolic ward setting with extreme dietary conditions.
- **Holder et al. (2019)**: Failed to replicate gut barrier disruption in more physiologically relevant human intestinal organoid models at food-relevant concentrations.

### Assessment
The Chassaing 2015 study was important for generating a hypothesis about synthetic emulsifiers and gut health, but it used extreme doses in an animal model. The evidence does not support a direct human health concern at food-additive levels. Regulatory authorities unanimously consider polysorbates safe.

### Recommendation: **LIMITED** ⬇ Downgrade
The mouse study evidence is too preliminary and at doses too far from human exposure to justify a 10-point penalty. Polysorbates are approved worldwide with generous ADIs, used even in injectable medicines. A limited (−3) penalty appropriately flags the ultra-processing signal without overstating the health risk. If human evidence emerges, upgrade to moderate.

## 3. E466 — Carboxymethyl Cellulose (CMC)

### Current classification: Moderate

### Regulatory status
- **JECFA**: "Not specified" ADI (most favorable). Reviewed multiple times, most recently reaffirmed.
- **EFSA (2018 re-evaluation)**: Maintained approval. No ADI set (considered so safe that no numerical limit was needed).
- **FDA**: GRAS. Used in foods, pharmaceuticals, cosmetics. Extensive safety record.

### Key evidence
- Same **Chassaing 2015** study as polysorbates. Same dose concerns (1% w/v, ~1,250 mg/kg bw/day in mice).
- **Chassaing et al. (2022, Gastroenterology)**: Human RCT with 16 healthy volunteers consuming 15 g/day CMC for 11 days in a metabolic ward. Found: reduced microbiome diversity, reduced short-chain fatty acids, some changes in fecal bacterial populations. However:
  - 15 g/day is at the very high end of human exposure (average intake estimated at 0.5-5 g/day)
  - Changes were modest and reversible
  - No clinical symptoms or biomarkers of inflammation were affected
  - Authors themselves noted the clinical relevance is uncertain
- **EFSA Panel (2023)** reviewed the Chassaing human data and concluded it did not change the safety assessment.

### Assessment
CMC actually has slightly more human evidence than polysorbate 80 (the 2022 Chassaing RCT). However, EFSA reviewed that evidence and was unimpressed. The human study showed subclinical microbiome shifts at high doses — interesting science, but not a demonstrated health risk. Both JECFA ("not specified" ADI) and EFSA consider CMC safe without numerical limits.

### Recommendation: **LIMITED** ⬇ Downgrade
Same logic as polysorbates. The 2022 human study is noteworthy but showed no clinical harm, and EFSA explicitly reviewed it without changing their assessment. CMC has a "not specified" ADI — the gold standard of safety in JECFA parlance. Limited (−3) is appropriate. If future studies show clinical effects, upgrade.

## 4. E338-E343, E450-E452 — Phosphates

### Current classification: Moderate

### Regulatory status
- **EFSA (2019)**: Re-evaluated phosphates as a group. Established a **group ADI of 40 mg/kg bw/day** (expressed as phosphorus). This was a *reduction* from the previous, more permissive assessment. EFSA noted that dietary exposure from food additives alone may approach or exceed this ADI in some population groups (children, adolescents).
- **FDA**: GRAS. Various phosphates approved for multiple functions. No ADI set by FDA.
- **JECFA**: MTDI of 70 mg/kg bw/day as phosphorus (higher than EFSA's ADI).

### Key evidence
- **Ritz et al. (2012, JASN)**: Higher serum phosphorus levels associated with increased cardiovascular mortality in the general population (not just CKD patients).
- **Dhingra et al. (2007, Archives of Internal Medicine)**: Framingham cohort; higher serum phosphorus associated with cardiovascular events even within the normal range.
- **Calvo & Uribarri (2013, Kidney International)**: Key review. Argued that inorganic phosphate additives are more bioavailable (~90-100% absorbed) than organic phosphorus in natural foods (~40-60% absorbed). Additive phosphorus has disproportionate impact on serum levels.
- **Chang et al. (2014, J Clin Endocrinol Metab)**: RCT showing phosphate-additive-enriched diet increased 24-hour urinary phosphorus and FGF23 levels compared to the same diet without additive phosphates.
- **EFSA (2019) risk assessment**: Concluded that exposure to phosphates from food additives is a safety concern, particularly for children, and reduced the group ADI.

### Assessment
This is NOT just about cumulative dietary phosphorus — the form matters. Inorganic phosphate additives are nearly 100% bioavailable, vs. ~40-60% for organic phosphorus in natural foods. EFSA took the unusual step of *reducing* the ADI in 2019. This is one of the few cases where a major regulatory body has become *more* concerned over time.

### Recommendation: **MODERATE** ✓ Keep
Strongly justified. EFSA reduced the ADI in 2019 — a meaningful regulatory signal. The cardiovascular epidemiology is substantial (Framingham data, multiple prospective cohorts). The bioavailability differential is well-established.

## 5. E280-E283 — Propionates (Propionic acid and salts)

### Current classification: Moderate

### Regulatory status
- **JECFA**: "Not specified" ADI (most favorable). Propionic acid is a normal human metabolic intermediate.
- **EFSA (2014 re-evaluation)**: Maintained approval. No safety concerns at current use levels. No numerical ADI needed.
- **FDA**: GRAS. Used in bread and baked goods as a mold inhibitor since the 1950s.

### Key evidence
- **Propionic acid is a normal human metabolite**: Produced endogenously by gut bacteria fermenting dietary fiber. Average endogenous production estimated at 300-600 mmol/day. Dietary additive exposure is a tiny fraction of this.
- **El-Ansary et al. (2012, 2014)**: Studies suggesting propionic acid injection (intracerebroventricular, in rats) could model autism-like behaviors. Route was direct brain injection, not oral. Doses were pharmacological. No epidemiological evidence linking dietary propionate to autism. EFSA explicitly dismissed these.
- **Tirosh et al. (2019, Science Translational Medicine)**: Propionate supplementation (1 g/day for 14 days) in humans altered glucose metabolism. But dose was ~10× higher than typical dietary additive exposure (~100 mg/day from bread). Short-duration, small study (n=14), not replicated.

### Assessment
Propionic acid has the strongest possible JECFA safety rating ("not specified" ADI). It's a normal human metabolite produced in gram quantities by gut bacteria. The concerning studies use non-dietary routes or doses far exceeding food-additive exposure.

### Recommendation: **LIMITED** ⬇ Downgrade
Propionate is endogenously produced, has a "not specified" ADI, and concerning studies used pharmacological doses or non-oral routes. Limited (−3) appropriately signals "processed food ingredient" without unwarranted health concern.

## 6. E508 — Potassium Chloride

### Current classification: Moderate (flagged as borderline limited)

### Regulatory status
- **JECFA**: "Not specified" ADI. Considered a normal dietary component.
- **EFSA**: Approved without restriction. Not even subject to the food additive re-evaluation program.
- **FDA**: GRAS. *Actively encouraged* by FDA as a sodium-reduction strategy.
- **WHO**: Recommends increased potassium intake (3.5 g/day) for adults to reduce blood pressure.
- **AHA**: Endorsed KCl-based salt substitutes in sodium reduction guidelines.

### Key evidence
- **Neal et al. (2021, NEJM)**: The SSaSS trial — 20,995 participants, 5 years. Replacing regular salt with 75% NaCl / 25% KCl reduced stroke by 14%, major cardiovascular events by 13%, and all-cause mortality by 12%.
- **GI effects**: Mild distress at high single doses — palatability issue, not toxicity.
- **Hyperkalemia risk**: Only relevant for severe renal impairment (eGFR <30) or potassium-sparing medications. Not a population-level concern.

### Assessment
Penalizing potassium chloride is actively counterproductive. The WHO recommends *increasing* potassium intake. The SSaSS trial showed it *saves lives*. Moderate classification penalizes products trying to be healthier.

### Recommendation: **LIMITED** ⬇ Downgrade
Risk_free would also be defensible, but limited (−3) acknowledges processing signal and rare kidney-disease awareness. Moderate (−10) is clearly too harsh for an ingredient the WHO recommends consuming more of.

## 7. E507 — Hydrochloric Acid

### Current classification: Moderate

### Regulatory status
- **JECFA**: "Not specified" ADI. Notes it is a normal constituent of gastric juice.
- **EFSA**: No safety concern. Classified as a processing aid / pH adjuster.
- **FDA**: GRAS. 21 CFR 184.1063. Used for pH adjustment in food manufacturing.

### Key evidence
- Hydrochloric acid is the primary component of human stomach acid. The stomach produces ~1.5-2 liters of gastric juice per day containing approximately 0.5% HCl.
- As a food additive, HCl is used in trace quantities to adjust pH during manufacturing. Fully neutralized by the food matrix and/or gastric buffering.
- There is **no scientific literature** suggesting harm from HCl as a food pH adjuster.
- The rationale ("safe at food-additive levels but a strong acid") describes a chemical property, not a health concern.

### Assessment
This is a classification error. Zero evidence base for penalizing HCl.

### Recommendation: **RISK_FREE** ⬇⬇ Downgrade (2 levels)
HCl is the same substance your stomach produces in large quantities. Penalizing it undermines the app's credibility with informed users.

## 8. E900 — Dimethylpolysiloxane (DMPS/PDMS)

### Current classification: Moderate

### Regulatory status
- **JECFA**: ADI of 0-1.5 mg/kg bw/day (established 1974, reaffirmed).
- **EFSA (2020 re-evaluation)**: Maintained approval. No safety concern. Noted very low oral bioavailability (<1% absorbed).
- **FDA**: GRAS. 21 CFR 173.340. Permitted as anti-foaming agent at up to 10 ppm.

### Key evidence
- **Bioavailability**: Essentially inert. <1% absorbed orally. Passes through GI tract unchanged.
- **Toxicology**: Extensive animal studies show no adverse effects even at doses far exceeding the ADI.
- **Exposure**: Typical dietary exposure 0.03-0.07 mg/kg bw/day — roughly 1/20th to 1/50th of the ADI.
- As noted: "Concern is more about ultra-processing signal than direct toxicity."

### Assessment
The moderate classification is driven by the "sounds scary" effect (silicone-based polymer in frying oil). The actual evidence profile is excellent: inert, unabsorbed, extensively studied, wide safety margin.

### Recommendation: **LIMITED** ⬇ Downgrade
As noted, the concern is ultra-processing signal, not direct toxicity. That's exactly what the limited tier is designed for.

## 9. E954 — Saccharin

### Current classification: Moderate

### Regulatory status
- **JECFA**: ADI of 5 mg/kg bw/day.
- **EFSA (2009)**: Maintained safety. No safety concern at current use levels.
- **FDA**: Delisted from NTP Report on Carcinogens in 2000. Congress repealed warning label in 2000 (PL 106-554).
- **IARC**: Downgraded from Group 2B to **Group 3** (not classifiable as carcinogenic) in 1999.
- **EPA**: Removed from hazardous substances list in 2010.

### Key evidence
- **Rat bladder cancer (1970s)**: Mechanism identified as rat-specific: sodium saccharin at high concentrations causes urinary silicate crystal precipitate that irritates rat bladder epithelium. Requires rat-specific urinary pH, α2u-globulin protein, and bladder physiology. Does not occur in mice, hamsters, primates, or humans.
- **Epidemiology**: Multiple large case-control studies (NCI, 1970s-1980s) found no association with bladder cancer in humans.
- **Delisting sequence**: IARC downgraded (1999) → NTP delisted (2000) → Congress repealed warning (2000) → EPA removed from hazardous list (2010).
- **Modern gut microbiome concerns**: Suez et al. (2014, Nature) included saccharin at extremely high doses in mice; human component was a 7-person non-randomized pilot. Insufficient to reclassify.

### Assessment
Saccharin's cancer scare is the most thoroughly debunked additive concern in history. Every major authority has reversed their initial caution.

### Recommendation: **LIMITED** ⬇ Downgrade
Limited (−3) is appropriate as an artificial sweetener signal (same tier as sugar alcohols). Moderate perpetuates a debunked narrative.

## 10. E220-E228 — Sulfites (9 entries)

### Current classification: Moderate

### Regulatory status
- **JECFA**: Group ADI of 0-0.7 mg/kg bw/day (as SO₂). Relatively tight ADI.
- **EFSA (2016 re-evaluation)**: ADI of 0.7 mg/kg bw/day. Noted ADI could be exceeded by high consumers of wine and dried fruits.
- **FDA**: GRAS, but with **mandatory labeling** at ≥10 ppm. One of FDA's 8 major sensitivity triggers. **Banned on raw fruits and vegetables** since 1986 after deaths in asthmatics.
- **Codex Alimentarius**: Requires labeling. Known allergen/sensitivity trigger.

### Key evidence
- **Sulfite sensitivity**: Well-documented, reproducible, clinically significant. ~1% general population, ~5% of asthmatics (Vally & Misso, 2012). Can cause severe bronchospasm, urticaria, angioedema, and (rarely) anaphylaxis.
- **Deaths**: FDA documented 13 deaths attributed to sulfite exposure (1982-1990).
- **Mechanism**: Well-characterized — sulfite oxidase deficiency, IgE-mediated and non-IgE-mediated pathways.
- **ADI exceedance**: EFSA noted high consumers may exceed the ADI. Meaningful since most ADIs have large safety margins.

### Assessment
Sulfites are unique in having **documented human fatalities** and a well-characterized adverse reaction mechanism. The ~5% prevalence among asthmatics is clinically significant. FDA mandates labeling specifically because the risk is real.

### Recommendation: **MODERATE** ✓ Keep
Strongly justified. Documented fatalities, mandatory FDA labeling, well-characterized sensitivity, tight ADI, EFSA exceedance concerns.

## 11. E231/E232/E233 — Fungicides

### Current classification: Moderate

### Regulatory status

**E231 (Orthophenyl phenol / OPP) & E232 (Sodium orthophenyl phenol / SOPP):**
- **JECFA**: ADI of 0-0.4 mg/kg bw/day (established, relatively low).
- **EFSA**: E231 and E232 were **withdrawn from the EU's approved food additive list** in 2004 (Directive 2003/114/EC). They are no longer authorized as food additives in the EU, though OPP remains authorized as a post-harvest pesticide on citrus fruit under separate pesticide regulations (with a different, tighter MRL framework).
- **FDA**: OPP is permitted as a post-harvest fungicide on citrus. Regulated under pesticide tolerances (40 CFR 180.129), not as a food additive per se.
- **EPA**: Classified OPP as a Group D carcinogen (not classifiable) in 1999. Has established tolerances for citrus fruit.
- **IARC**: OPP was evaluated in 1999; classified as Group 3 (not classifiable as carcinogenic to humans), but noted that SOPP caused bladder tumors in male rats (species-specific mechanism involving urinary pH).

**E233 (Thiabendazole / TBZ):**
- **JECFA**: ADI of 0-0.1 mg/kg bw/day. This is an unusually tight ADI, reflecting concern about hepatotoxicity and teratogenicity at higher doses.
- **EFSA**: Like E231/232, **removed from the EU food additive list**. Still permitted as a post-harvest pesticide under pesticide MRL regulation.
- **FDA**: Approved as a post-harvest fungicide for citrus and bananas. Also used as an anthelmintic drug in humans (Mintezol) at therapeutic doses (25 mg/kg bw/day) — 250× the JECFA food ADI.
- **EPA**: Classified as a likely carcinogen at high doses based on liver tumors in mice.

### Key evidence
- **EU withdrawal**: The fact that the EU removed these from the approved food additive list is itself a strong regulatory signal. They were deemed unnecessary as food additives when they're already regulated as pesticides.
- **OPP bladder tumors**: Male rat bladder tumors via a mechanism involving high urinary pH and sodium salt precipitation — similar to saccharin's rat-specific mechanism. IARC classified as Group 3.
- **TBZ teratogenicity**: Demonstrated in animal studies at doses above the NOAEL. The JECFA ADI of 0.1 mg/kg bw/day is one of the tightest in the food additive database, reflecting real toxicological concern.
- **TBZ hepatotoxicity**: Liver enzyme elevation documented even at therapeutic doses in humans. The wide gap between the food ADI (0.1 mg/kg) and the therapeutic dose (25 mg/kg) provides a safety margin, but the underlying liver toxicity is real.
- **Residue reality**: These are surface-applied to citrus fruit peels. Actual consumer exposure via food is very low if the fruit is peeled, higher if the peel is consumed (marmalade, zesting, candied peel).

### Assessment
These are legitimate moderate-risk substances. They are functional pesticides that the EU felt were inappropriately listed as "food additives." E233 (TBZ) has a genuinely tight ADI reflecting real hepatotoxicity and teratogenicity signals. The EU's decision to de-list them from the food additive framework (while keeping them as regulated pesticides) is a meaningful distinction — it says "these should be treated as pesticide residues with MRLs, not as food ingredients."

### Recommendation: **MODERATE** ✓ Keep
Justified. EU de-listed from food additive framework. TBZ has one of the tightest ADIs in the database (0.1 mg/kg bw/day) with documented hepatotoxicity and teratogenicity. OPP is less concerning on its own (IARC Group 3), but the regulatory treatment as pesticide rather than food additive is a meaningful signal. The group classification at moderate is appropriate.

## 12. E150c/E150d — Ammonia Caramel (Class III) / Sulphite Ammonia Caramel (Class IV)

### Current classification: Moderate

### Regulatory status
- **JECFA**: Group ADI of 0-200 mg/kg bw/day for E150c and E150d (based on the caramel itself, not 4-MEI specifically). This is a generous ADI.
- **EFSA (2011 re-evaluation)**: Maintained approval but set a specific limit for **4-MEI (4-methylimidazole)** at 250 mg/kg in the caramel color. EFSA also noted that the group ADI could be exceeded by high consumers of cola-type beverages and soy sauce.
- **FDA**: Approved. No specific 4-MEI limit at the federal level. FDA stated in 2014 that 4-MEI levels in foods are not an immediate safety concern.
- **California OEHHA (Prop 65)**: Listed 4-MEI as a carcinogen in 2011. Set a NSRL (No Significant Risk Level) of 29 μg/day. This triggered Coca-Cola and Pepsi to reformulate their caramel color suppliers to reduce 4-MEI in US products.
- **NTP (2007)**: 2-year bioassay found "clear evidence of carcinogenicity" for 4-MEI in male and female mice (lung tumors) and "some evidence" in female rats.

### Key evidence
- **4-MEI (4-methylimidazole)** is the specific contaminant of concern. It's a process byproduct formed during the manufacture of Class III (ammonia) and Class IV (sulphite ammonia) caramel colors. It is NOT present in Class I (E150a, plain caramel) or Class II (E150b, caustic sulphite caramel).
- **NTP bioassay (2007)**: Clear evidence of lung carcinogenicity in mice at doses of 312 and 625 ppm in feed. The lowest dose showing clear effects (312 ppm) corresponds to roughly 40-50 mg/kg bw/day in mice.
- **Human exposure**: Estimated at 0.1-0.4 μg/kg bw/day from cola consumption (post-reformulation). This is approximately **100,000× lower** than the mouse carcinogenic dose. However, OEHHA uses a linear low-dose extrapolation model that doesn't assume a threshold.
- **California Prop 65 impact**: The NSRL of 29 μg/day is extremely conservative. A single can of reformulated Coke contains ~4 μg of 4-MEI (down from ~100+ μg pre-reformulation). The Prop 65 listing drove industry reformulation, which actually reduced exposure.
- **IARC**: Has NOT formally evaluated 4-MEI as of 2024. There is no IARC classification.
- **Distinction from E150a/E150b**: The current system correctly classifies E150a (plain caramel) as risk_free and E150b (caustic sulphite) as limited. The 4-MEI concern is specific to the ammonia-process caramels.

### Assessment
The 4-MEI concern is real but requires nuance. The NTP found clear evidence of carcinogenicity in mice. California listed it under Prop 65. However, human exposure is many orders of magnitude below animal carcinogenic doses, even for heavy cola drinkers. The industry has already reformulated to reduce 4-MEI.

The key argument for moderate: the NTP bioassay is authoritative, Prop 65 listing is a meaningful regulatory signal (even if conservative), and E150d is ubiquitous in processed foods (cola, soy sauce, baked goods, gravies). Cumulative exposure matters. The fact that industry reformulated in response suggests the concern was taken seriously.

The key argument against moderate: human exposure is ~100,000× below animal effect levels; EFSA maintained a generous ADI; IARC hasn't classified it; no human epidemiological evidence of harm.

On balance, moderate is defensible. The NTP evidence is real, the contaminant is specific and measurable, and the regulatory response (Prop 65, industry reformulation) was proportionate.

### Recommendation: **MODERATE** ✓ Keep
Justified. NTP clear evidence of carcinogenicity, Prop 65 listing, and the fact that the concern is about a specific measurable contaminant (4-MEI) rather than vague "it might be bad" makes this a well-founded moderate classification. The correct distinction between E150a (risk_free) and E150c/d (moderate) is already in place.

## 13. E620/E622-E625 — Glutamic Acid and Glutamate Salts

### Current classification: Moderate (5 entries: E620, E622, E623, E624, E625)

### Regulatory status
- **JECFA**: "Not specified" ADI for glutamic acid and its salts as a group (established 1987). This is the most favorable safety designation.
- **EFSA (2017 re-evaluation)**: Established a group ADI of 30 mg/kg bw/day for glutamic acid and glutamates (E620-E625). This was a *new* numerical limit where none previously existed. However, EFSA explicitly stated this was based on **neurodevelopmental toxicity in animals at very high doses** and that current dietary exposure from food additives is generally within the ADI.
- **FDA**: GRAS. FDA commissioned a FASEB review (1995) which concluded that MSG and glutamates are safe for the general population at normal consumption levels. "Chinese Restaurant Syndrome" was not confirmed as a reproducible phenomenon in double-blind trials.
- **FASEB (1995)**: The most comprehensive independent review. Concluded: (1) MSG is safe at normal dietary levels; (2) "MSG symptom complex" may occur in some individuals consuming ≥3 g MSG on an empty stomach, but this is a transient sensitivity, not a health hazard; (3) no evidence of neurotoxicity at dietary doses.

### Key evidence
- **Glutamic acid is the most abundant amino acid in the human diet.** Natural glutamate is present at high levels in tomatoes (~250 mg/100g), parmesan cheese (~1,200 mg/100g), soy sauce (~1,000 mg/100g), and human breast milk (~22 mg/100mL). The body cannot distinguish between glutamate from food additives and glutamate from tomatoes.
- **"Chinese Restaurant Syndrome" debunked**: Multiple double-blind, placebo-controlled studies (Geha 2000; Tarasoff & Kelly 1993; Mortensen 2000) failed to reproducibly demonstrate symptoms from MSG in controlled settings. The original Kwok 1968 letter was anecdotal and likely reflected publication bias.
- **Neurotoxicity (Olney, 1969)**: John Olney's work showing glutamate neurotoxicity in neonatal mice used subcutaneous injection of massive doses (0.5-4 g/kg), bypassing the GI tract. Dietary glutamate does not reach the brain at these concentrations because:
  - The gut metabolizes ~95% of ingested glutamate during first-pass
  - The blood-brain barrier actively regulates glutamate entry
  - Plasma glutamate levels rise only modestly after oral intake
- **EFSA's 2017 ADI of 30 mg/kg bw/day**: Based on a NOAEL from developmental neurotoxicity studies in rodents, applying a 100× safety factor. EFSA acknowledged this was "a conservative approach" and noted that "the effects observed in animals occurred at doses considerably higher than the estimated dietary exposure."
- **Note on E621 (MSG) vs. E620-E625**: E621 (MSG itself) is currently classified as **high** in the seed data, while E620 and E622-E625 are moderate. This is inconsistent. MSG is the sodium salt of glutamic acid; E622-E625 are potassium, calcium, ammonium, and magnesium salts. They are toxicologically equivalent. E621 is a candidate for reclassification.

### Assessment
The evidence is overwhelming: glutamic acid and its salts are safe at food-additive levels. JECFA gave them the strongest possible safety designation ("not specified" ADI). EFSA's 2017 numerical ADI was precautionary and conservative, and exposure is generally within it. The "Chinese Restaurant Syndrome" narrative has been debunked in controlled trials. Glutamate is a normal and abundant component of the human diet.

The moderate classification appears to be inherited from cultural bias ("MSG is bad") rather than evidence. The additive documentation correctly identifies this as problematic for E621 (MSG) at high, but the same logic applies to E620/E622-E625 at moderate.

### Recommendation: **LIMITED** ⬇ Downgrade
Glutamates are naturally present in high concentrations in many foods. JECFA's "not specified" ADI and FASEB's comprehensive safety determination make moderate unjustified. Limited (−3) is appropriate as an ultra-processing signal — added glutamates indicate flavor enhancement in processed food — without perpetuating the debunked "MSG is dangerous" narrative.

**Note**: This also strengthens the case for reclassifying E621 (MSG) from high to limited, which additive documentation already flags. The distinction between E621 and E620-E625 is purely the counterion (Na vs. K/Ca/NH₄/Mg) and has zero toxicological significance.

## 14. E627/E631/E635 — Nucleotide Flavor Enhancers

### Current classification: Moderate (3 entries)

### Regulatory status
- **JECFA**: "Not specified" ADI for disodium guanylate (E627) and disodium inosinate (E631) (established). The most favorable safety designation.
- **EFSA (2015)**: Re-evaluated E627, E631, and E635. Concluded there was **no safety concern** at current dietary exposure levels. Did not set a numerical ADI (maintained the "not specified" / "not limited" approach).
- **FDA**: GRAS. Used as flavor enhancers, typically in combination with MSG to produce synergistic umami flavor.

### Key evidence
- **What they are**: Disodium guanylate (GMP) and disodium inosinate (IMP) are purine nucleotides. E635 is a commercial blend of the two. They occur naturally in many foods:
  - IMP: abundant in meat and fish (particularly aged/dried meats)
  - GMP: present in mushrooms (especially shiitake — the traditional source of umami)
  - Both are normal metabolic intermediates in purine metabolism
- **Purine/gout concern (the basis for moderate classification)**:
  - Purines are metabolized to uric acid. Elevated uric acid causes gout in susceptible individuals.
  - However, dietary exposure from E627/E631/E635 as food additives is **tiny** compared to purine intake from natural foods. These are used at levels of 10-50 mg per serving. A single serving of meat contains 100-300 mg of purines; a serving of organ meat (liver) contains 300-600 mg.
  - EFSA's 2015 assessment explicitly noted that the contribution of nucleotide food additives to total purine intake is negligible (estimated at <1% of total dietary purine intake).
  - There is **no epidemiological study** linking E627/E631/E635 intake to gout incidence.
- **Typical usage levels**: 10-50 mg per serving, always in combination with glutamates. These are potency enhancers — a tiny amount dramatically boosts umami perception, so very little is needed.
- **Allergic/sensitivity concerns**: None documented. Well-tolerated.

### Assessment
The gout concern is theoretically valid (purines → uric acid → gout) but quantitatively negligible. The amount of purine from nucleotide flavor enhancers is <1% of total dietary purine intake. JECFA gave them the strongest possible safety rating. EFSA found no safety concern. There are no human studies showing harm. Penalizing these more heavily than naturally purine-rich foods (meat, mushrooms, beer) is not evidence-based.

### Recommendation: **LIMITED** ⬇ Downgrade
The purine/gout concern is quantitatively irrelevant at food-additive exposure levels. JECFA "not specified" ADI, EFSA "no safety concern." Limited (−3) is appropriate as an ultra-processing signal (presence of nucleotide flavor enhancers indicates engineered flavor in processed food). Moderate is disproportionate.
