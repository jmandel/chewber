# E262 (Sodium acetates) Research Report

## Identity
- **E-number:** E262
- **Component forms:**
  - **E262(i):** sodium acetate
  - **E262(ii):** sodium hydrogen diacetate (sodium diacetate)
- **CAS numbers:**
  - Sodium acetate (anhydrous): **127-09-3**
  - Sodium acetate trihydrate: **6131-90-4**
  - Sodium hydrogen diacetate (sodium diacetate): **126-96-5**
- **Chemical class:** salts/acid salts of acetic acid (short-chain fatty acid derivative; acidity regulator/preservative class)
- **Common synonyms:** sodium acetate, sodium ethanoate, sodium hydrogen diacetate, sodium diacetate
- **Natural vs synthetic:** both naturally occurring and synthetic routes are used; acetate is a normal dietary/metabolic constituent, while commercial material is manufactured industrially.

## Function in Food
- **Primary technological role:** acidity regulator / pH control.
- **Secondary roles:** antimicrobial preservative (especially sodium diacetate), flavoring agent/adjuvant.
- **Mechanism of action:** acetate systems reduce pH and increase antimicrobial activity of undissociated acetic acid species; they also provide acid-salt buffering behavior.
- **Common food categories (from CFR use limits):** baked goods, fats/oils, grain products/pastas, snack foods, hard/soft candy, jams/jellies, meat products, soups/soup mixes, gravies/sauces, sweet sauces, breakfast cereals.

## Regulatory Status
### EFSA (EU)
- **Status:** authorized in EU; re-evaluation workflow entries for E262(i) and E262(ii) are currently marked **"Ongoing Risk Assessment"** in OpenEFSA, with EFSA comments stating the additive is already authorized and re-evaluation is delayed.
- **Latest relevant published EFSA scientific output found:** **EFSA Journal 2022;20(6):7351** (buffered vinegar, primary constituents acetic acid/acetates).
- **ADI:** EFSA notes prior conclusion (2013) that establishing an ADI for acetic acid/acetate is **not considered necessary**.
- **Key conclusions (2022 opinion):** no safety concern at proposed maximum/typical uses for buffered vinegar; could not conclude for quantum satis use because exposure could not be estimated.

## Key Safety Evidence
### Regulatory and review-level evidence
- EFSA 2022 (buffered vinegar, acetic acid/acetate-based) concluded no safety concern at proposed maximum/typical use levels, while not concluding for quantum satis uses due to non-estimable exposure.
- FDA CFR keeps sodium acetate and sodium diacetate in GRAS-affirmed Part 184 with CGMP and category-specific levels.
- JECFA distinguishes ADI outcomes between 262(i) and 262(ii) records (not limited vs 0-15 mg/kg bw).

Sources:
- EFSA 2022 PMID 35795292: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35795292/
- CFR sections listed above
- JECFA records listed above

### PubMed recent safety studies
- **Food Chem (2018, PMID 29622200):** in HUVEC in vitro models, sodium acetate/sodium diacetate showed concentration-time dependent cytotoxicity; authors concluded safe use at low concentrations.
- **J Biochem Mol Toxicol (2018, PMID 30019796):** isolated lymphocyte in vitro assays reported concentration-dependent cytotoxicity and DNA damage with sodium acetate.
- **Food Sci Biotechnol (2025, PMID 39958174):** human lymphocyte in vitro assays reported increased micronucleus/chromosomal aberration metrics at higher concentrations of sodium acetate.
- **Food Funct (2017, PMID 29090701):** sodium diacetate showed condition-dependent toxicity in non-human cell systems, with stronger effects under acidic conditions.

### Animal and human data context
- Older JECFA monographs characterize acetate as part of normal metabolism and report no adverse-effect concerns at typical dietary context, while describing toxicity at large doses and irritancy potential for concentrated acetic systems.
- I did not identify robust recent epidemiological evidence linking normal dietary E262 exposure to specific chronic disease outcomes.

## Exposure Assessment
- EFSA 2022 buffered-vinegar exposure estimates (as acetic acid equivalents):
  - Mean: **8.9 mg/kg bw/day (infants)** to **280.3 mg/kg bw/day (children)**
  - 95th percentile: **27.9 mg/kg bw/day (infants)** to **1,078 mg/kg bw/day (toddlers)**
- EFSA still concluded no safety concern for proposed maximum/typical use levels in that application.
- For E262 specifically, a unified numeric ADI is not straightforward across authorities because JECFA entries differ between 262(i) and 262(ii), while EFSA’s acetate framing indicates ADI not necessary.
- **Potentially vulnerable groups:** toddlers/children with high processed-food intake, and sodium-sensitive individuals (because sodium load is a broader dietary concern even if acetate toxicology signal is low at normal use).

## Risk Assessment
### 1. Tier-by-tier analysis

#### `risk_free`
- **Evidence supporting this tier:**
  - Acetate is an endogenous metabolite and normal dietary constituent (JECFA/EFSA context).
  - EFSA cites prior conclusion that ADI establishment for acetate/acetic acid was not necessary.
  - JECFA lists sodium acetate (262(i)) ADI as "not limited".
- **Evidence against this tier:**
  - JECFA has a separate sodium hydrogen diacetate (262(ii)) entry with numeric ADI (0-15 mg/kg bw), not "not limited".
  - Multiple in vitro studies (2018, 2025) report cytotoxic/genotoxic signals at higher concentrations.

#### `limited`
- **Evidence supporting this tier:**
  - FDA GRAS-affirmed with specific use conditions (21 CFR Part 184).
  - EFSA 2022 found no safety concern at proposed maximum/typical levels for a related acetate-based additive scenario.
  - No clear IARC carcinogenic classification identified for sodium acetate/diacetate.
  - No major bans found in reviewed regulatory sources.
- **Evidence against this tier:**
  - Some mechanistic/in vitro studies show adverse cellular effects at high concentrations.
  - EFSA did not endorse quantum satis use for buffered vinegar due to exposure uncertainty.

#### `moderate`
- **Evidence supporting this tier:**
  - High-end exposure estimates in young groups can be substantial in specific application scenarios.
  - In vitro genotoxicity/cytotoxicity signals create a non-zero mechanistic concern, especially for high local concentrations.
- **Evidence against this tier:**
  - Regulatory bodies continue to permit use under defined conditions.
  - No strong in vivo or epidemiological evidence of harm at real-world dietary exposure levels was identified.

#### `high`
- **Evidence supporting this tier:**
  - Limited: isolated in vitro findings at high concentration.
- **Evidence against this tier:**
  - No major-jurisdiction bans found.
  - No identified IARC Group 1/2A/2B classification for the E262 substances.
  - EFSA/FDA/JECFA frameworks remain permissive under specified conditions.

### 2. Rationale
The weight of evidence is strongest for regulatory acceptance with controlled use limits and weak for severe hazard classification. Signals of cellular stress/genotoxicity are mostly in vitro and concentration-dependent, and currently do not appear to outweigh the broader regulatory toxicology context for normal food-use conditions.

### 3. Recommended tier
- **Recommended tier:** `limited`
