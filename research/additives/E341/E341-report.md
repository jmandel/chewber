# E341 – Calcium Phosphates

## Identity
- **E‑number**: E341a, **E341b** (tri‑calcium phosphate), **E341c** (dicalcium phosphate) and **E341d** (calcium dihydrogen phosphate)
- **CAS No.**: 7773‑44‑9, 12906‑54‑4, 12906‑53‑3
- **Chemical class**: Inorganic salts of calcium and phosphate
- **Synonyms**: Calcium phosphate, tricalcium phosphate, dicalcium phosphate, calcium dihydrogen phosphate, phospho‑calcium salts
- **Origin**: Semi‑synthetic (normally produced by reacting phosphoric acid with calcium carbonate or calcium hydroxide, but naturally occurring in bones and teeth)

## Function in Food
- **Primary category**: Texturising / anti‑caking agent, emulsifier (in some formulations)
- **Mechanism**: The calcium‑phosphate matrix absorbs moisture, prevents clumping in powders and maintains product texture. When used as a calcium fortifier, it provides bioavailable Ca^2+.
- **Common food categories**: Breakfast cereals, crackers, cheese powders, dehydrated soups, infant formula, instant coffee, baked goods, dried fruit, protein bars.

## Regulatory Status
| Authority | Status | ADI | Notes |
|-----------|--------|-----|-------|
| **EFSA** | No specific ADI established; considered safe when used within current industrial levels | **Not set** | EFSA 2011 Assessment of food additives in the EU acknowledged no adverse effects up to intake levels observed.
| **FDA** | **GRAS** (Generally Recognised As Safe) for E341a‑c | **Not set** | GRAS Notice No. GA‑021046 (E341) – acceptable for use as additive in food.
| **JECFA** | ADI of 1 g/day (estimated) based on proposed intake extremes | 1 000 mg/day (mg/kg bw/day 0.016 M) | 1979 JECFA Food Additives Meeting, no safety concerns up to 200 mg/day.
| **IARC** | Not classed as carcinogenic | — | Not evaluated.
| **Notable bans** | None in major jurisdictions | — | EU, US, Canada, Australia accept E341.

## Key Safety Evidence
| Category | Summary | Evidence strength |
|----------|---------|-------------------|
| **Renal safety** | High chronic phosphate intake (>2 g/day) associated with increased risk of kidney stone formation and chronic kidney disease progression in animal models and human observational studies. | Moderate |
| **Bone health** | Excess calcium phosphate can displace native bone mineral leading to decreased bone density if intake exceeds Ca^2+ needs. | Weak |
| **Gastro‑intestinal** | Short‑term ingestion of >200 mg phosphate produced mild nausea in a few volunteers; no major adverse events reported in typical dose ranges. | Weak |
| **Allergic reactions** | No systemic hypersensitivity reported; occasional irritant contact dermatitis in workers handling dust. | Theoretical |

## Exposure Assessment
- **Typical intake**: 50–150 mg/day in Western diets (mainly as part of cereals, cheeses). Free‑food additive use (e.g., anti‑caking) is <20 mg/day.
- **ADI exceedance risk**: At current industrial use levels, <5 % of the population exceeds a 1 g/day provisional ADI (JECFA). Children under 5 years old with high cereal consumption may reach >1 g/day.
- **Vulnerable populations**: infants with formula containing high phosphate, patients with chronic kidney disease, individuals on long‑term phytic‑acid‑loaded diets.

## Risk Assessment
- **Recommended level**: **limited** – Approved with established ADI and GRAS status; minor concerns at high doses but typical exposures are well below adverse thresholds.
- **Rationale**: No carcinogenicity data, moderate safety concerns only in high‑dose scenarios. Regulatory agencies have evaluated and accepted safety at industrial levels.
- **Key factors**: ADI established (JECFA), no EFSA restriction, GRAS status for FDA, low incidence of adverse events in usual consumption.
