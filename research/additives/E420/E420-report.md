# E420 (Sorbitol) Research Report

## Identity
- **E-number:** E420 (E420(i) sorbitol; E420(ii) sorbitol syrup) [1][2][3]
- **Primary name:** D-sorbitol [3]
- **CAS numbers:**
  - 50-70-4 (sorbitol / D-sorbitol) [3][2]
  - 68425-17-2 (sorbitol solution, from the CFR definition) [2]
- **Chemical class:** sugar alcohol (polyol; hexitol) [3]
- **Common synonyms:** D-glucitol, Sorbol, Sorbit [3]
- **Natural vs synthetic:** naturally occurring in some foods, but commercial food-grade sorbitol is manufactured by catalytic hydrogenation of glucose/corn syrup/hydrolyzed starch [2]

## Function in Food
- **Core technological roles:** sweetener, humectant, bulking agent, texturizer, stabilizer, thickener, sequestrant [2][3]
- **Mechanism of action:** polyol hydroxyl groups bind water (humectancy) and provide bulk/sweetness with osmotic effects at higher doses (relevant to GI tolerance) [2][6]
- **Common food categories:** commonly used in confectionery and sweet processed foods (e.g., candies/chocolates/biscuits/jams) and in reduced-sugar formulations [6]

## Regulatory Status
- **EFSA (EU):**
  - Status shown as **Authorised**; E420 re-evaluation is marked **ongoing** on EFSA sweeteners topic page [1].
  - Latest EFSA scientific action located: 2023 call requesting technical/toxicological data for E420(i)/(ii), including new genotoxicity data; EFSA notes no genotoxicity concern is expected if requested in-vitro micronucleus results are negative [4].
  - **ADI (EFSA):** no final EFSA re-evaluation ADI identified on currently available EFSA pages reviewed; re-evaluation still in progress [1][4].
- **FDA / eCFR (US):**
  - **GRAS status:** listed under 21 CFR Part 184 (Direct Food Substances Affirmed as Generally Recognized as Safe), section **21 CFR 184.1835 Sorbitol** [2].
  - **Conditions of use:** may be safely used as a nutritive sweetener, humectant, sequestrant, and texturizer in food, consistent with good manufacturing practice [2].
- **JECFA / WHO:**
  - WHO JECFA database entry for D-sorbitol (INS 420(i)) lists **ADI: NOT SPECIFIED** and **Evaluation year: 1982** [3].
- **IARC:**
  - In IARC Monographs classification data (last update shown in data feed: 2026-02-25), **sorbitol itself is not listed as an IARC-classified agent**; a related but different substance, iron sorbitol-citric acid complex, appears as Group 3 [5].
- **Notable bans:** no major-jurisdiction ban found in the reviewed EFSA/FDA/JECFA/IARC sources [1][2][3][5].

## Key Safety Evidence
- **Animal toxicology (signal):** a 2025 rat study reported dose-dependent renal injury biomarkers and histopathology after sub-chronic sorbitol exposure (20/45/90 mg/kg) [6].
  - Interpretation: mechanistic concern signal exists, but this is single-study evidence and not by itself a regulatory consensus.
- **Gut/microbiome intolerance mechanism:** 2024 Cell study reported persistent sorbitol intolerance in mice after antibiotic + high-fat context, linked to reduced microbial sorbitol catabolism (Clostridia depletion) [7].
  - Interpretation: supports biological plausibility for GI intolerance in susceptible contexts.
- **Liver mechanistic model:** 2025 Science Signaling zebrafish work showed microbiome depletion increased sorbitol transfer and steatotic liver phenotype, including risk signal under high exogenous sorbitol in that model [8].
  - Interpretation: plausible pathway in model systems; not direct proof of population-level food-additive harm.
- **Human observational data:** 2025 cohort analysis associated sorbitol intake during early life with lower BMI-z and microbiome differences [9].
  - Interpretation: observational and potentially confounded; does not establish causality or safety at all doses.
- **Human clinical tolerance (non-food-preference context):** 2025 colonoscopy preparation studies using D-sorbitol combinations reported favorable short-term tolerability/safety [10][11].
  - Interpretation: supportive for short-term medical use, but not directly equivalent to long-term dietary exposure patterns.

## Exposure Assessment
- **Regulatory intake benchmark:** JECFA ADI is **not specified** for D-sorbitol [3].
- **ADI exceedance risk:** a numeric exceedance analysis is limited because no numeric ADI was identified for current JECFA listing, and EFSA re-evaluation is ongoing [1][3][4].
- **Practical risk pattern:** risk appears to be driven more by **dose and susceptibility** (GI intolerance context, microbiome state, high-bolus intake) than by a low chronic toxicological threshold currently recognized by regulators [7][8].
- **Potentially vulnerable populations:**
  - people with sorbitol/FODMAP intolerance or functional bowel sensitivity,
  - individuals after major microbiome disruption (e.g., recent high-impact antibiotic exposure, based on animal mechanistic evidence),
  - high consumers of polyol-rich products in short windows [7][10][11].

## Risk Assessment
### 1. Tier-by-tier analysis
- **risk_free**
  - Evidence supporting this tier:
    - Long-standing regulatory acceptance in US/EU context and JECFA ADI not specified [1][2][3].
    - No IARC carcinogenic classification for sorbitol itself in current IARC list [5].
  - Evidence against this tier:
    - EFSA re-evaluation is still ongoing with targeted data requests [1][4].
    - Recent mechanistic/animal signals (renal injury model; intolerance and liver mechanisms in model organisms) argue against a blanket "risk-free" label [6][7][8].
- **limited**
  - Evidence supporting this tier:
    - FDA GRAS (21 CFR 184.1835) with GMP conditions and EU authorized status [1][2].
    - JECFA ADI not specified and no direct carcinogenic classification for sorbitol [3][5].
    - Human short-term medical-use tolerability data are generally favorable [10][11].
  - Evidence against this tier:
    - Nontrivial susceptibility-dependent GI/metabolic concerns in recent mechanistic literature [7][8].
    - One recent animal study reports nephrotoxicity signals [6].
- **moderate**
  - Evidence supporting this tier:
    - EFSA re-evaluation still unresolved and newer mechanistic findings justify caution for susceptible groups [1][4][7][8].
    - A contemporary toxicology paper raises renal concern at tested doses [6].
  - Evidence against this tier:
    - No widespread regulatory restrictions or bans in major jurisdictions from reviewed sources [1][2][3].
    - No IARC Group 2A/2B assignment for sorbitol itself [5].
- **high**
  - Evidence supporting this tier:
    - No strong supporting evidence found.
  - Evidence against this tier:
    - Not banned in major reviewed frameworks; no IARC high-concern classification for sorbitol itself; regulators continue to allow use [1][2][3][5].

### 2. Rationale
The evidence is most consistent with **approved use plus dose/susceptibility caveats**: regulatory bodies continue to permit sorbitol, while newer literature highlights possible adverse effects in specific biological contexts and at higher or non-typical exposures. This pattern fits a controlled-risk profile rather than either risk-free or high-risk classification.

### 3. Recommended tier
**limited**
