# E624 (Monoammonium glutamate) Research Report

## Identity
- **E-number:** E624
- **Name:** Monoammonium glutamate (JECFA: monoammonium L-glutamate)
- **CAS number(s):** 7558-63-6 (WHO/JECFA entry for INS 624)
- **Chemical class:** Ammonium salt of L-glutamic acid (amino-acid salt)
- **Common synonyms:** Monoammonium L-glutamate monohydrate; ammonium glutamate
- **Natural vs synthetic:** Glutamic acid is naturally present in foods and produced in the body, but E624 is used as a manufactured additive salt form in foods [1][5].

## Function in Food
- **Primary function:** Flavour enhancer (umami/savoury note) [5][6].
- **Additional function:** Salt substitute role is listed in JECFA functional classes [5].
- **Mechanism of action:** Studies including MAG (monoammonium glutamate) show glutamate-based enhancers intensify salty/umami perception and can help maintain salty taste in sodium-reduced formulations [6][7].
- **Common food categories:** EFSA identifies broad additive uses and specifically highlighted fine bakery wares, soups/broths, sauces, meat products, seasonings/condiments, and food supplements when discussing exposure-management needs [1].

## Regulatory Status
### EFSA (EU)
- **Approval status:** EFSA describes glutamates (E620-E625, including E624) as authorised food additives in the EU [1].
- **Latest evaluation:** 2017 re-evaluation (EFSA Journal 2017;15(7):4910) [1][2].
- **ADI:** Group ADI of **30 mg/kg bw/day** for glutamic acid + glutamates (E620-E625) [1].
- **Key conclusions:**
  - EFSA set a new group ADI and stated this level is below doses associated with certain human effects (e.g., headache, raised blood pressure, increased insulin) [1].
  - Refined exposure estimates indicated exceedance risk: high consumers in all age groups, and even medium exposure in toddlers/children could exceed ADI; highly exposed infants/children/adolescents could exceed doses associated with some adverse effects [1].
  - EFSA recommended reviewing maximum permitted levels [1].
- **Access note:** Direct retrieval of the EFSA Journal article page was blocked in this environment by anti-bot/CAPTCHA protection; EFSA press release and linked metadata were accessible [1][2][3].

### FDA / CFR (U.S.)
- **GRAS status:** Monoammonium glutamate is listed as GRAS in **21 CFR 182.1500** [4].
- **CFR citation:** **21 CFR 182.1500(a)-(b)** [4].
- **Conditions of use:** "generally recognized as safe when used in accordance with good manufacturing practice" [4].
- **Access note:** Direct interactive eCFR pages can challenge automated access; eCFR API content endpoint was accessible and provided the full regulatory text [4].

### JECFA / WHO
- **Substance-specific entry:** WHO/JECFA entry for **MONOAMMONIUM L-GLUTAMATE (INS 624)** [5].
- **ADI:** **Not specified** (group ADI comment covering L-glutamic acid and its ammonium/calcium/sodium/magnesium/potassium salts) [5].
- **Last evaluation year listed:** **1987** [5].
- **Related group context:** WHO/JECFA sodium glutamate entry also states ADI not specified with the same group framing [8].

### IARC
- No dedicated IARC monograph classification for monoammonium glutamate was identified from the IARC "Agents Classified" page and the cumulative cross-index search performed here [9][10].

### Notable bans
- No major-jurisdiction ban for E624 was identified in the regulatory sources reviewed here [1][4][5].

## Key Safety Evidence
### Animal studies (recent)
- **Neurotoxicity model:** Rat study (2024) using MSG 600 mg/kg/day for 28 days reported neurotoxicity markers and histological brain changes; green tea co-treatment partially mitigated findings [11].
- **Reproductive toxicity model:** Rat study (2024) reported that MSG (1500 mg/kg) worsened LPS-associated sperm, hormonal, oxidative-stress, and histological outcomes [12].
- **Immune/inflammation model:** Rat study (2026) reported inflammatory/tissue alterations with chronic MSG dosing over 90 days, with partial probiotic effects in some groups [13].

### Human and clinical interpretation
- A major review (2019) concluded many adverse-effect reports rely on high-dose or methodologically weak designs, limiting extrapolation to typical dietary exposure [14].
- A more recent review focused on food allergy implications (2024) reported little consistent evidence for true IgE-mediated MSG allergy at realistic dietary intakes, while noting occasional intolerance-like reports in susceptible individuals [15].

### Mechanistic concerns
- Animal toxicology literature frequently implicates oxidative stress, inflammatory signaling, and endocrine changes at higher-dose exposures; translation to real-world dietary intake remains uncertain [11][12][13][14].

## Exposure Assessment
- EFSA's refined assessment (industry use data + analytical data + consumption data) found potential ADI exceedance in multiple groups, especially high consumers and younger populations [1].
- EFSA also stated that total glutamate exposure (additive + other dietary sources) can exceed both the proposed ADI and levels associated with some adverse effects in several medium-to-high exposure groups [1].
- Specific E624-only intake data are limited in these sources; risk characterisation is typically performed on the **group** of glutamate additives (E620-E625) [1][5].
- **Vulnerable populations flagged by EFSA:** infants, toddlers, children, and adolescents at higher exposure scenarios [1].

## Risk Assessment
### 1. Tier-by-tier analysis
#### risk_free
- **Evidence supporting risk_free:**
  - JECFA lists ADI as "not specified" for the glutamate-salt group, which can indicate low concern at normal use [5][8].
  - Glutamic acid itself is naturally present in foods and endogenous in humans [1].
- **Evidence arguing against risk_free:**
  - EFSA set a finite group ADI (30 mg/kg bw/day) and identified likely exceedance in several populations [1].
  - Recent animal studies continue to report adverse findings under higher-dose conditions [11][12][13].

#### limited
- **Evidence supporting limited:**
  - EFSA/EU and FDA frameworks continue to permit use (authorised/GRAS) with GMP and risk-management context [1][4].
  - Human-allergy evidence appears weak/inconsistent at realistic intake in reviewed literature [14][15].
- **Evidence arguing against limited:**
  - EFSA explicitly raised exceedance concerns and recommended re-review of maximum levels [1].
  - Multiple recent animal studies show biologically plausible toxicity signals at elevated doses [11][12][13].

#### moderate
- **Evidence supporting moderate:**
  - Approved additive, but with a defined ADI and documented exceedance risk in some populations (especially children/high consumers) [1].
  - Consistent animal hazard signals (neuro, reproductive, inflammatory endpoints) in high-dose/chronic paradigms [11][12][13].
  - Uncertainty remains for human relevance, but not enough to dismiss concerns entirely [14][15].
- **Evidence arguing against moderate:**
  - JECFA's long-standing group ADI-not-specified position and continued regulatory acceptance in major systems [4][5][8].

#### high
- **Evidence supporting high:**
  - High-dose animal studies report serious endpoint changes [11][12][13].
- **Evidence arguing against high:**
  - No major bans identified and no IARC carcinogenic classification found for this additive [4][5][9][10].
  - EFSA did not conclude inability to establish safe use; it set an ADI and recommended exposure controls [1].

### 2. Rationale
E624 sits in a regulated-caution profile: it remains permitted, but EFSA's 2017 re-evaluation introduced a group ADI and explicitly flagged population-level exceedance risk, particularly in younger/high-intake groups. Recent animal findings reinforce biological plausibility for harm at elevated doses, while human evidence at realistic intake is less consistent.

### 3. Recommended tier
**moderate**
