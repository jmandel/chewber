## Function in Food

**Mechanism of action:** Triethyl citrate functions as a carrier solvent, sequestrant (metal-ion chelator), emulsifier, foam stabilizer, plasticizer, and flavoring agent/adjuvant. In egg white products, it binds and neutralizes residual egg yolk, enabling compressed whipped egg white to maintain stable foam structure. As a sequestrant, it chelates metal ions (Ca²⁺, Fe²⁺/³⁺) that could otherwise destabilize emulsions or cause off-flavors. As a carrier solvent, it dissolves and delivers flavoring compounds and other food additives.

**Common food categories:**
- Whipped egg white products (primary EU use)
- Chewing gum and confectionery
- Beverage flavorings
- Sauces and dressings
- Desserts and baked goods
- Pharmaceutical tablet coatings (indirect food contact)
- Food packaging materials (as plasticizer — migrates at low levels)

## Key Safety Evidence

### Metabolism & Pharmacokinetics

Triethyl citrate is rapidly hydrolyzed in the body by liver and serum esterases into **citric acid** (a normal Krebs cycle intermediate) and **ethanol**. Rat serum hydrolyzes TEC within 15 minutes; human serum hydrolysis is slower but still occurs within hours (Figdor & Ballinger, 1981). Both rat and human liver homogenates completely hydrolyze TEC to 1 mol citric acid + 3 mol ethanol per mol ester (Burns & Werners, 1962). The metabolic products are endogenous substances with well-characterized safety profiles.

### Acute Toxicity

- Oral LD50 in rats: >5,000 mg/kg bw (very low acute toxicity)
- Relatively nontoxic in acute studies

### Subchronic and Chronic Toxicity

- **2-year rat feeding study:** NOAEL ≥ 2,000 mg/kg bw/day (highest dose tested = 4% in diet). No treatment-related adverse effects observed. No organ toxicity. This is the pivotal study for the JECFA ADI.
- Differences from controls in various feeding studies were either not statistically significant or not related to organ toxicity (CIR 2002 review).

### Genotoxicity

- **Not genotoxic** in bacterial (Ames) or mammalian test systems (CIR 2002; FSCJ 2015).
- FSCJ concluded: "triethyl citrate has no genotoxicity relevant to human health."

### Carcinogenicity

- **No carcinogenicity** demonstrated. No significant differences in tumor induction in 2-year rat studies (CIR 2002; FSCJ 2015).
- Not evaluated by IARC.

### Reproductive and Developmental Toxicity

- No reproductive or developmental toxicity in available studies (FSCJ 2015).
- Acetyl tributyl citrate (related compound) was not a developmental or reproductive toxicant in mice and rats (CIR 2002).

### Dermal and Sensitization

- Not toxic via dermal application in rabbits.
- Guinea pig maximization test: Acetyl triethyl citrate (related ester) showed some sensitization, but clinical testing in humans was negative for both irritation and sensitization (CIR 2002).
- CIR Expert Panel (2023 update) confirmed acetyl triethyl citrate and related citrates are safe as cosmetic ingredients.

### Emerging Research — Potential Neurotoxicity (Computational)

- **Ouyang et al. (2025):** A network toxicology and molecular docking study identified 229 theoretical neurotoxicity targets for TEC, suggesting potential interactions with MAPK3, SRC, CASP3, TNF, and BCL2 proteins. The authors concluded TEC "may affect key processes such as cell death, inflammation, and neuronal health" and recommended further research. **This is a computational/in-silico study only — no in vivo or in vitro neurotoxicity was demonstrated.** Evidence strength: weak/theoretical.
  - PMID: 40273610

### Zebrafish Developmental Studies

- **Yun et al. (2024):** Compared ATBC (acetyl tributyl citrate) and ATEC (acetyl triethyl citrate) in zebrafish larvae. ATEC showed **no significant effects on development or locomotor behavior** at any tested concentration (0.03–300 μg/L), while ATBC caused growth inhibition, behavioral changes, and endocrine disruption. This study is favorable for the safety profile of triethyl citrate esters.
  - PMID: 37977359

### Intravenous Effects (Not Relevant to Dietary Exposure)

- IV administration of acetyl triethyl citrate/acetyl tributyl citrate causes dose-related decreases in blood pressure and intestinal spasms. This route is not relevant to food use.

## Exposure Assessment

**Typical dietary intake:**
- Japan FSCJ estimated 2.3 mg/kg bw/day (127 mg/person/day) if approved for all food uses
- The JECFA ADI of 0–20 mg/kg bw/day provides a substantial margin of safety (~8.7-fold above estimated Japanese intake)
- In the EU, primary authorization is limited to egg white products (carrier solvent use), so actual dietary exposure is likely very low
- In the US, GRAS status with GMP limits means use levels are self-regulated by industry

**ADI exceedance risk:**
- Very low. Even the relatively generous Japanese intake estimate of 2.3 mg/kg bw/day is well below the ADI of 20 mg/kg bw/day
- The NOAEL (2,000 mg/kg bw/day) provides a ~870-fold margin above estimated intake
- For children: No specific concerns identified. Proportional intake may be higher per kg body weight, but still well within safety margins given the very high NOAEL

**Vulnerable populations:**
- No specific vulnerable populations identified in regulatory assessments
- Individuals with rare esterase enzyme deficiencies might theoretically metabolize TEC more slowly, but no clinical cases of adverse effects have been reported

## Risk Assessment

### Tier-by-Tier Analysis

#### risk_free
**Evidence supporting this tier:**
- FSCJ (Japan, 2015) concluded "it is not necessary to specify the ADI" — the strongest possible safety endorsement, essentially equivalent to "ADI not specified"
- Metabolized to endogenous substances (citric acid + ethanol) — both are normal body metabolites
- Occurs naturally in plant and animal tissues
- NOAEL ≥ 2,000 mg/kg bw/day with no adverse effects at the highest tested dose
- No genotoxicity, no carcinogenicity, no reproductive/developmental toxicity
- No safety concerns identified by any regulatory body worldwide
- CIR Expert Panel confirmed safety for cosmetic use (topical) in 2002 and 2023
- Zebrafish study (2024) showed no effects at any concentration tested

**Evidence against this tier:**
- JECFA established a numerical ADI (0–20 mg/kg bw/day) rather than "ADI not specified," suggesting some residual caution
- One computational study (2025) raised theoretical neurotoxicity concerns, though no experimental evidence supports this
- It is a synthetic ester, not purely a naturally occurring substance in the quantities used

#### limited
**Evidence supporting this tier:**
- JECFA ADI of 0–20 mg/kg bw/day is an established numerical limit (not "not specified")
- GRAS/approved with long safety history but technically has a defined ADI

**Evidence against this tier:**
- The ADI is based on the highest dose tested (NOAEL = highest dose), meaning no actual toxicity was found even at extreme doses
- FSCJ's more recent and comprehensive 2015 evaluation found ADI specification unnecessary
- No safety signals of any kind at realistic exposure levels

#### moderate
**Evidence supporting this tier:**
- Computational neurotoxicity study (2025) raised theoretical concerns
- EFSA re-evaluation is still pending (no formal modern EFSA opinion)

**Evidence against this tier:**
- No actual experimental evidence of harm at any dose in any species
- No ADI reductions, no regulatory restrictions, no credible safety concerns
- The neurotoxicity study is purely in-silico with no supporting in vivo data
- Universal regulatory approval with no caveats

#### high
**Evidence supporting this tier:**
- None.

**Evidence against this tier:**
- No bans in any jurisdiction
- No IARC classification
- No genotoxicity or carcinogenicity
- No evidence of ADI exceedance
- Approved and affirmed safe by FDA, JECFA, FSCJ, Health Canada, and the EU

### Rationale

Triethyl citrate has an exceptionally strong safety profile. It is metabolized to endogenous substances (citric acid and ethanol), occurs naturally in small amounts, and has shown no adverse effects even at the highest doses tested in chronic studies (2,000 mg/kg bw/day in 2-year rat studies). The JECFA ADI of 0–20 mg/kg bw/day was set at the highest tested dose divided by 100, not because toxicity was observed. Japan's FSCJ conducted the most recent comprehensive evaluation (2015) and concluded ADI specification was unnecessary. No genotoxicity, carcinogenicity, reproductive toxicity, or organ toxicity has been demonstrated. The only note of caution is a single 2025 computational study suggesting theoretical neurotoxic interactions, but this has no experimental confirmation and is considered weak evidence. All major regulatory bodies approve its use without significant restrictions.

### Recommended tier: **risk_free**
