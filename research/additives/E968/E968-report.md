## Function in Food

**Primary function:** Sweetener (bulk, non-nutritive)

**Mechanism of action:** Erythritol provides sweetness at approximately 60–70% the intensity of sucrose with essentially zero calories. Unlike most sugar alcohols, erythritol is rapidly absorbed in the small intestine (>90%) and excreted unchanged in urine within 24 hours, rather than being fermented in the colon. This unique metabolic fate explains its superior gastrointestinal tolerance compared to other polyols (sorbitol, maltitol, xylitol) — at typical doses it does not reach the large intestine in sufficient quantities to cause osmotic diarrhea.

**Secondary functions:** Humectant, flavor enhancer, formulation aid, stabilizer and thickener, sequestrant, texturizer, carrier solvent.

**Common food categories:**
- Sugar-free and reduced-calorie beverages
- Chewing gum
- Baked goods and baking mixes
- Frozen dairy desserts
- Hard and soft candies
- Sugar substitutes / tabletop sweeteners
- Dairy products and dairy analogs
- Jams, jellies, and fruit preparations
- Snack foods
- Condiments and sauces
- Breakfast cereals

## Regulatory Status

### EFSA (European Food Safety Authority)

- **Status:** Approved (authorised in the EU in 66 food categories, 83 uses, at quantum satis levels)
- **ADI:** 0.5 g/kg bw/day (500 mg/kg bw/day)
  - Basis: Lower bound of NOAELs for diarrhoea in human studies
  - This was the **first time** EFSA established a numerical ADI for erythritol (the SCF in 2003 had not set one)
  - Also the first time an ADI was derived for any food additive based on an immediate adverse effect (diarrhoea)
- **Evaluation history:**
  - 2003: SCF evaluation — declared safe, no numerical ADI set
  - 2010, 2013, 2015: EFSA opinions on extensions of use
  - **2023: Full re-evaluation completed** (adopted 25 October 2023, published 20 December 2023)
- **Opinion reference:** EFSA Journal 2023;21(12):e8430 (DOI: 10.2903/j.efsa.2023.8430)
- **Key conclusions:**
  1. Not genotoxic
  2. Can cause diarrhoea at high doses (classified as adverse due to electrolyte/water imbalance risk)
  3. Exposure estimates exceed ADI in all population groups (though considered overestimations due to conservative modelling)
  4. No causal link demonstrated to cardiovascular disease
  5. No short-term effect on glucose homeostasis
  6. Laxative warning label ("excessive consumption may produce laxative effects") remains appropriate
  7. Application for laxative warning exemption was **rejected**
  8. Recommended lowering lead limit in EU specifications
- **Source:** https://www.efsa.europa.eu/en/plain-language-summary/re-evaluation-erythritol-e-968-food-additive
- **Source:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38125972/

- **GRAS Notice Numbers:** GRN 76 (2001), GRN 208 (2006), GRN 382 (2011), GRN 401 (2012), GRN 789 (2019)

### JECFA (Joint FAO/WHO Expert Committee on Food Additives)

- **ADI:** "Not specified" — JECFA's most favorable safety classification, meaning no numerical limit is needed because erythritol is of very low toxicity
- **Last evaluation year:** 1999 (53rd JECFA meeting)
- **Key findings:**
  - Not carcinogenic, not genotoxic, no reproductive/developmental toxicity
  - In humans, 1 g/kg bw/day in foods for 5 days caused no effects
  - Rapidly absorbed and excreted unchanged in urine — not metabolized
  - No effect on glucose or insulin
  - Animal NOELs were mostly 1–2 g/kg bw/day, but effects were physiological (osmotic), not toxic
- **Report:** TRS 896-JECFA 53/18
- **Source:** https://apps.who.int/food-additives-contaminants-jecfa-database/Home/Chemical/961
- **Source:** https://www.inchem.org/documents/jecfa/jecmono/v44jec03.htm

### IARC (International Agency for Research on Cancer)

- **Classification:** None — erythritol has **never been evaluated or classified by IARC** for carcinogenicity
- **Source:** https://monographs.iarc.who.int/list-of-classifications/ (searched; 0 results for "erythritol")

### Notable Bans

- None. Erythritol is approved in all major markets including EU, USA, Japan, Canada, Australia/New Zealand, and China.

## Key Safety Evidence

### Animal Studies

- **Chronic toxicity / carcinogenicity:** Long-term feeding studies in rats and mice at doses up to 5% of diet showed no carcinogenic, mutagenic, or teratogenic effects (JECFA 1999 review).
- **Reproductive/developmental toxicity:** No adverse effects observed (JECFA 1999).
- **Genotoxicity:** Negative in all standard battery tests (Ames, chromosomal aberration, in vivo micronucleus). Confirmed by EFSA 2023 re-evaluation.
- **Thrombosis (animal models):** Witkowski et al. (2023) demonstrated faster clotting time in mice injected with erythritol at physiological concentrations.

### Epidemiological / Clinical Data

- **Cardiovascular risk (Witkowski et al., 2023, Nature Medicine):** In untargeted metabolomics of 1,157 patients undergoing cardiac risk assessment, circulating erythritol levels were associated with incident 3-year risk for major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE). Validated in US (n=2,149) and European (n=833) cohorts — fourth vs. first quartile adjusted HR: 1.80 (1.18–2.77) and 2.21 (1.20–4.07) respectively. PMID: 36849732.
- **Platelet reactivity (Witkowski et al., 2024, ATVB):** Prospective interventional study in healthy volunteers (n=10 per group). Dietary erythritol (30 g) caused >1000-fold increase in plasma erythritol concentration and enhanced stimulus-dependent platelet aggregation in all subjects and agonists tested. Glucose (30 g) did not. PMID: 39114916.
- **Counterpoint (Mazi & Stanhope, 2023, Nutrients):** Narrative review noting that patients with inborn errors of metabolism who have chronically elevated erythritol do not exhibit higher platelet activation or thrombosis risk. Most long-term animal studies do not support a role in platelet activation. Elevated erythritol may be a marker of pentose phosphate pathway dysregulation rather than a causal factor. PMID: 37764794.
- **Editorial (Witkowski & Hazen, 2025, Eur Heart J):** Authors argue that erythritol and xylitol cardiovascular concerns represent a "growing concern" that warrants regulatory re-evaluation. PMID: 39565321.

### Gastrointestinal Effects

- Erythritol has the best GI tolerance of all polyols due to >90% small intestinal absorption. However, at high doses (≥0.5–1 g/kg bw), it can cause osmotic diarrhoea.
- EFSA (2023) classified diarrhoea as an adverse effect (rather than just a nuisance) due to the risk of clinically significant electrolyte and water imbalance, especially in children.
- The threshold for laxation varies: adults tolerate ~0.5–0.8 g/kg bw; children may be more sensitive.

### Mechanistic Concerns

- **Platelet activation:** At plasma concentrations achievable from dietary intake (>40 μM), erythritol enhanced platelet responsiveness to multiple agonists in vitro (ADP, collagen, TRAP6).
- **Thrombosis:** In vivo mouse model showed accelerated thrombus formation.
- **Endogenous production:** Humans naturally produce small amounts of erythritol (~2–4 mg/dL plasma) via the pentose phosphate pathway. Dietary intake causes a >1000-fold transient increase.

### Confirmed Non-Concerns

- **Genotoxicity:** Negative across all standard tests (EFSA 2023, JECFA 1999)
- **Carcinogenicity:** No evidence of carcinogenic potential (JECFA 1999, EFSA 2023)
- **Reproductive/developmental toxicity:** No adverse effects (JECFA 1999)
- **Glucose/insulin response:** No effect — safe for diabetics (JECFA 1999)
- **Dental caries:** Noncariogenic; may actually have anti-cariogenic properties

## Exposure Assessment

- **Typical dietary intake:** Variable; depends heavily on consumption of sugar-free/reduced-calorie products. Can be significant in individuals using erythritol-based tabletop sweeteners (e.g., Swerve, Truvia baking blend).
- **EFSA exposure estimates (2023):** Conservative modelling suggested ADI (0.5 g/kg bw/day) exceedance in all population groups, though EFSA noted these were likely overestimates due to the assumption that erythritol is used at maximum levels in all permitted categories.
- **Single-dose context:** A 70 kg adult's ADI = 35 g/day. A single can of erythritol-sweetened beverage may contain 5–15 g. The Witkowski interventional study used a 30 g dose.
- **ADI exceedance risk:** Moderate, particularly for:
  - Children (lower body weight, higher per-kg consumption)
  - Individuals consuming multiple sugar-free products daily
  - People using erythritol as a primary sugar substitute in cooking/baking
- **Vulnerable populations:** Children (more susceptible to GI effects at lower doses), individuals with pre-existing cardiovascular disease (theoretical concern based on Witkowski et al. findings).

## Risk Assessment

### Tier-by-Tier Analysis

#### risk_free
**Evidence supporting this tier:**
- JECFA assigned "ADI not specified" (most favorable classification, 1999)
- Naturally occurring in fruits and produced endogenously by humans
- Not genotoxic, not carcinogenic, no reproductive toxicity
- Excellent gastrointestinal tolerance relative to other polyols
- Decades of safe use in Japan (since 1990), EU, and USA
- No bans in any jurisdiction
- FDA GRAS with five independent "no questions" determinations

**Evidence against this tier:**
- EFSA 2023 set a numerical ADI of 0.5 g/kg bw/day (indicating the substance is not entirely without concern)
- Credible 2023–2024 evidence linking erythritol to enhanced platelet reactivity and thrombosis potential
- EFSA exposure estimates suggest ADI exceedance in multiple population groups
- Diarrhoea classified as adverse effect, not merely nuisance

#### limited
**Evidence supporting this tier:**
- Approved worldwide with established safety record
- EFSA ADI of 0.5 g/kg bw/day is relatively generous (35 g/day for 70 kg adult)
- Cardiovascular concerns are recent and remain debated — observational associations may reflect reverse causation (erythritol as marker of metabolic dysregulation)
- EFSA 2023 concluded no causal link to cardiovascular disease demonstrated
- GI effects only at high doses
- Counterarguments to cardiovascular concerns published (Mazi & Stanhope 2023)

**Evidence against this tier:**
- The cardiovascular signal, while debated, comes from a major journal (Nature Medicine) with mechanistic support (platelet activation in vitro, thrombosis in animal models, confirmed enhancement of platelet reactivity in healthy human volunteers)
- EFSA's newly established ADI suggests the pre-2023 consensus of "essentially harmless" has shifted

#### moderate
**Evidence supporting this tier:**
- EFSA set a new numerical ADI in 2023 (previously no ADI was deemed necessary)
- EFSA exposure modelling suggests ADI exceedance across all population groups
- Credible cardiovascular concern from Nature Medicine publication (2023) with mechanistic support
- Follow-up interventional study (2024) confirmed platelet reactivity enhancement in healthy volunteers
- Children identified as potentially vulnerable population for both GI and exposure exceedance
- The evidence base is evolving rapidly and additional studies are ongoing

**Evidence against this tier:**
- The cardiovascular link is associative, not yet proven causal
- EFSA itself concluded no causal link to CVD demonstrated
- The ADI exceedance modelling was acknowledged as conservative/overestimated
- Long clinical and commercial use history without detected cardiovascular signal in the general population
- Patients with chronically elevated endogenous erythritol (inborn errors of metabolism) don't show elevated thrombosis

#### high
**Evidence supporting this tier:**
- None that meets the criteria. Not banned anywhere, no IARC classification, no EFSA "unable to confirm safety" finding, no bioaccumulation.

**Evidence against this tier:**
- All major regulators continue to approve erythritol
- EFSA 2023 re-evaluation confirmed safety within the ADI
- No carcinogenicity, no genotoxicity

### Rationale

Erythritol occupies a space between "limited" and "moderate" risk. It has a strong traditional safety profile — naturally occurring, endogenously produced, FDA GRAS, JECFA "ADI not specified," and decades of safe commercial use. However, the 2023 EFSA re-evaluation marked a notable shift by establishing a numerical ADI for the first time, driven by GI effects at high doses. More significantly, the Witkowski et al. findings (2023 in Nature Medicine, 2024 in ATVB) represent a credible new safety signal linking dietary erythritol to enhanced platelet reactivity and thrombosis potential, supported by epidemiological association, in vitro data, animal models, and a human interventional study.

The key tension is: EFSA reviewed these cardiovascular findings and concluded no causal link was demonstrated, while the study authors and editorialists argue the evidence warrants regulatory re-evaluation. The "moderate" tier is appropriate because (a) EFSA newly established a numerical ADI suggesting the substance is not risk-free, (b) the cardiovascular signal has mechanistic backing even if causation isn't proven, (c) ADI exceedance is plausible in some populations, and (d) the situation is actively evolving. However, the concerns fall short of "high" since no jurisdiction has restricted erythritol and the cardiovascular evidence is still debated.

### Recommended Tier: **moderate**
