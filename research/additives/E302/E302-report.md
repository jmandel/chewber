# E302 (Calcium ascorbate) - Research Report

## Identity
- **E-number**: E302 (INS 302) [S5]
- **Name**: Calcium ascorbate [S5]
- **CAS number(s)**: 5743-27-1 (calcium ascorbate dihydrate) [S5]
- **Chemical class**: Ascorbate salt (calcium salt of L-ascorbic acid; antioxidant) [S5][S6]
- **Common synonyms**: Calcium ascorbate dihydrate; calcium salt of 2,3-didehydro-L-threo-hexono-1,4-lactone dihydrate; L-ascorbic acid calcium salt (2:1); calcium L-ascorbate dihydrate [S5][S13]
- **Natural vs synthetic**: Commercial E302 is used as a manufactured food additive, but the active moiety is the calcium salt form of vitamin C (ascorbate). (Inference from additive status + chemical identity) [S3][S5]

## Function in Food
- **Primary function**: Antioxidant [S5]
- **Mechanism of action**: As an ascorbate salt, it acts as a reducing antioxidant that limits oxidative reactions and browning in foods. (Inference from antioxidant functional class plus food-application studies) [S5][S10][S11]
- **Common food categories reported in recent literature**:
  - Fresh-cut vegetables/fruits (e.g., potatoes, apples, broccoli stalk products) [S10][S12]
  - Processed meat systems (e.g., marinated beef, bacon-related formulations) [S11]

## Regulatory Status
- **EFSA (EU)**:
  - **Latest food-additive re-evaluation located**: 2015 EFSA Journal opinion on E300/E301/E302 [S1]
  - **ADI**: No numerical ADI needed (i.e., no mg/kg bw/day value set) [S1]
  - **Key conclusion**: No safety concern at reported uses/use levels for ascorbic acid and salts (including E302) [S1]
  - **Status**: Interpreted as approved for food-additive use under existing EU framework (based on re-evaluation outcome). [S1]
- **FDA / CFR (US)**:
  - **Status**: GRAS when used per GMP [S3]
  - **Citation**: **21 CFR 182.3189 (Calcium ascorbate)** [S3]
  - **Condition of use**: "generally recognized as safe when used in accordance with good manufacturing practice." [S3]
- **JECFA / WHO**:
  - **ADI**: "NOT SPECIFIED" (group ADI for ascorbic acid and sodium/potassium/calcium salts) [S5][S6]
  - **Evaluation year**: 1981 [S5]
- **IARC**:
  - No listing for calcium ascorbate/ascorbate terms in the current IARC classified-agents dataset checked (last update shown 2026-02-25), so no IARC group assignment identified for E302 specifically. [S7][S8]
- **Notable bans**:
  - No major-jurisdiction ban identified in the sources reviewed. [S1][S3][S5]

## Key Safety Evidence
- **JECFA toxicological monograph (calcium ascorbate)**:
  - Reports low toxicity profile in available animal/human data reviewed at the time; non-mutagenic findings were reported in cited test systems. [S6]
  - Committee noted oxalate as a major ascorbate metabolite and discussed potential stone risk at very large intakes, but did **not** consider special restrictions necessary for food-additive/nutritional use. [S6]
  - ADI conclusion: not specified. [S6]
- **EFSA food additive re-evaluation (2015)**:
  - No numerical ADI required and no safety concern at reported uses/use levels. [S1]
- **Recent EFSA context (feed additive renewal, 2025)**:
  - Additives as vitamin C sources remained safe for target species/consumers/environment in renewal context.
  - For **user/handler safety**, panel reported insufficient data to conclude specifically for calcium ascorbate. (Occupational data gap, not a consumer-food exposure signal.) [S9]
- **Recent human literature**:
  - Preliminary RCT (2024) comparing ascorbic acid vs calcium ascorbate in healthy adults did not report major acute safety alarms in study outcomes. [S10]
  - 2025 systematic review reported no major tolerability concerns for calcium ascorbate-containing formulations in healthy adults, while noting need for more research. [S11]

## Exposure Assessment
- **Intake context**:
  - JECFA historical monograph text estimated daily ascorbic acid intake from natural sources at ~30-100 mg/day (historical estimate). [S6]
  - NHANES values cited by NIH ODS: mean vitamin C intakes from food/beverages were 105.2 mg/day (adult males) and 83.6 mg/day (adult females) in 2001-2002 data. [S12]
- **High-intake context**:
  - NIH ODS UL for adults is 2,000 mg/day vitamin C; high intakes can cause GI effects, and kidney-stone risk is discussed mainly for high supplemental intakes/at-risk individuals. [S12]
- **ADI exceedance risk for E302**:
  - EFSA/JECFA did not establish a numerical ADI for this additive group; therefore a numeric ADI exceedance calculation is not applicable in the standard way.
  - Practical concern is more about very high total vitamin C exposure from supplements than typical food-additive exposure under GMP. [S1][S3][S5][S12]
- **Potentially vulnerable groups**:
  - Individuals prone to nephrolithiasis/renal disorders under high vitamin C exposure scenarios; people with iron-overload conditions at chronically high supplemental intakes. [S6][S12]

## Risk Assessment
### 1. Tier-by-tier analysis
- **risk_free**
  - Supporting evidence:
    - JECFA ADI not specified; EFSA no numerical ADI needed and no safety concern at reported uses. [S1][S5][S6]
    - U.S. CFR status is GRAS under GMP for direct food use. [S3]
  - Evidence against:
    - Some uncertainty remains for very high total vitamin C intakes (renal/oxalate discussion), and recent EFSA feed-user data gaps exist for handler exposure. [S6][S9][S12]
- **limited**
  - Supporting evidence:
    - Broad approval/GRAS profile with no major consumer safety signal at food-use levels.
    - Minor concerns mainly arise at high supplemental doses or non-consumer exposure contexts. [S1][S3][S6][S9][S12]
  - Evidence against:
    - Regulatory ADI status is "not specified," which often argues for a lower concern profile than a typical "limited" category.
- **moderate**
  - Supporting evidence:
    - Not strongly supported; no clear evidence of population-level harm from regulated food-additive use in reviewed sources.
  - Evidence against:
    - No EFSA/JECFA finding of unsafe use at reported levels; no IARC Group 2A/2B signal identified for E302. [S1][S5][S7][S8]
- **high**
  - Supporting evidence:
    - None identified (no major-jurisdiction ban, no IARC carcinogenic group assignment for E302 found).
  - Evidence against:
    - Multiple authorities indicate low concern at normal food-use conditions. [S1][S3][S5][S6]

### 2. Rationale
Calcium ascorbate sits in a low-risk regulatory position (EFSA/JECFA no numerical ADI; U.S. GRAS/GMP). Residual concerns are mostly conditional: very high total vitamin C exposure (especially supplements) and data gaps for certain user/handler endpoints, rather than a demonstrated consumer hazard from normal additive use.

### 3. Recommended tier
**limited** (conservative placement due to high-dose and user-safety caveats despite an overall low-risk profile).
