# E1420 Research Report: Acetylated starch

## Identity
- **E-number:** E1420 (INS 1420) [1][2]
- **Name:** Acetylated starch (starch acetate) [2][3]
- **CAS number(s):** 9045-28-7 (starch acetate) [3]
- **Chemical class:** Chemically modified starch (acetylated starch ester) [2][3]
- **Common synonyms:** Acetylated starch, starch acetate, INS 1420, food starch-modified (U.S. regulatory naming context) [2][3][4]
- **Origin (natural vs synthetic):** **Semi-synthetic** (derived from food starch and chemically acetylated) [2][5]

## Function in Food
- **Mechanism of action:** Acetylation introduces acetyl groups that reduce intermolecular reassociation (retrogradation) and alter hydration/rheology, improving stability and processing functionality in formulated foods [5].
- **Regulatory functional class:** Emulsifier, stabilizer, thickener (JECFA modified starches entry including starch acetate) [3].
- **Common food categories:** Broad processed-food use under GMP/authorized conditions where thickening, stabilization, or emulsification is needed (U.S. 21 CFR 172.892 framework does not set a narrow category list for food starch-modified) [4].

## Regulatory Status

### EFSA (EU)
- **Status:** Approved/authorized in EU (re-evaluated within the modified starch group including E1420) [1][2].
- **ADI:** EFSA reports ADI context as **"not specified"** and concludes there is **no need for a numerical ADI** for modified starches including E1420 [2].
- **Evaluation year:** 2017 (EFSA Journal opinion doi:10.2903/j.efsa.2017.4911) [1][2].
- **Key conclusion:** No safety concern for reported uses/use levels for the general population [2].

### FDA / CFR (U.S.)
- **GRAS status:** FDA substance entry for `FOOD STARCH-MODIFIED` lists regulatory status as **Direct Food Substances Affirmed as GRAS** [6].
- **CFR citation:** **21 CFR 172.892** (`Food starch-modified`) [4][6].
- **Conditions of use (key points):**
  - Food starch-modified "may be safely used in food" under listed treatment limits [4].
  - For acetylation routes, acetyl groups in finished food starch-modified are limited to **not more than 2.5%** [4].
  - Reagent/residual limits are specified (e.g., phosphorus oxychloride/acetic anhydride or vinyl acetate combinations; residual phosphate limits) [4].
  - FDA substance entry reports: **"No limitations other than current good manufacturing practice"** [6].

### JECFA / WHO
- **ADI:** **NOT SPECIFIED** (listed in 2021/2016/1982 entries on the modified starches chemical page) [3].
- **Last evaluation year shown:** 2021 (comment: revised for specification only; ADI remains NOT SPECIFIED) [3].
- **Identity link to E1420:** JECFA modified starches page includes CAS 9045-28-7 for starch acetate [3].

### IARC
- **Classification:** No IARC monograph classification was found for "starch acetate"/"acetylated starch" in the IARC monographs site searches performed [7][8].

## Key Safety Evidence
- **Regulatory toxicology synthesis (EFSA 2017):**
  - Modified starches (including E1420) were considered not genotoxic based on in silico analysis.
  - No treatment-related effects relevant for human risk assessment were observed in rats up to 31,000 mg/kg bw/day.
  - EFSA concluded no safety concern at reported uses and no need for a numerical ADI [2].

- **Chronic animal evidence (1986 mouse study):**
  - 89-week feeding study included starch acetate at high dietary inclusion levels.
  - Reported GI/renal-adaptive findings (e.g., caecal/colonic enlargement, some urinary/renal findings), and **no evidence of carcinogenicity** for products tested [10].

- **Subchronic study (2015 rat study on cassava starch acetate film):**
  - 90-day oral exposure reported no obvious toxicity in hematology, biochemistry, renal parameters, organ weights, or histopathology in that model [9].

- **Recent literature landscape (PubMed):**
  - Recent acetylated-starch publications are largely structure/function/technology-focused rather than new long-term human safety epidemiology for food-additive E1420 [5][8].

## Exposure Assessment
- **EFSA group-level modified starch exposure:**
  - EFSA reported combined exposure (E1404-E1451) at the 95th percentile refined scenario up to **3,053 mg/kg bw/day** (group-level, not E1420-only) [2].
  - EFSA summary page also reports highest refined exposures in younger age groups (especially toddlers), with lower values in adults/elderly [1].

- **E1420-specific intake quantification:**
  - A separate quantitative intake estimate for E1420 alone was not identified in the accessed regulatory pages; available figures were generally for combined modified starches [1][2].

- **ADI exceedance risk:**
  - EFSA and JECFA use "ADI not specified"/no numerical ADI framing, so classic exceedance-versus-numeric-ADI calculations are not directly applicable [2][3].

- **Potentially vulnerable populations:**
  - High consumers in younger age groups (e.g., toddlers) have the highest group-level modified-starch exposure estimates in EFSA's assessment scenarios [1][2].

## Risk Assessment

### 1. Tier-by-tier analysis
- **risk_free**
  - Evidence supporting this tier: EFSA and JECFA both maintain non-numerical ADI (not specified) context; EFSA concluded no safety concern at reported uses [2][3].
  - Evidence arguing against this tier: E1420 is a chemically modified additive (not native starch), and modern E1420-specific long-term human datasets are limited; older high-dose animal findings show biological effects even if not clearly adverse at real-world exposures [5][10].

- **limited**
  - Evidence supporting this tier: EU and U.S. frameworks permit use; EFSA no-safety-concern conclusion; JECFA ADI not specified; no IARC listing found [1][2][3][4][6][7].
  - Evidence arguing against this tier: compound-specific recent chronic epidemiology is sparse, and much recent literature is functionality-focused rather than hazard-focused [5][8].

- **moderate**
  - Evidence supporting this tier: historic high-dose animal studies reported GI/renal-adaptive effects; EFSA group exposure is highest in toddlers, which justifies attention to high-consumption subgroups [1][10].
  - Evidence arguing against this tier: regulatory bodies still conclude safety at reported uses and have not set restrictive numerical ADIs for this group [2][3][4].

- **high**
  - Evidence supporting this tier: no supporting evidence found (no major-jurisdiction ban, no IARC Group 1/2A/2B classification located, no EFSA/JECFA withdrawal) [2][3][7].
  - Evidence arguing against this tier: current international regulatory consensus remains permissive with specified manufacturing/use conditions [1][2][3][4][6].

### 2. Rationale
The strongest signals are consistent regulator conclusions (EFSA/JECFA/FDA-CFR framework) indicating low concern at current uses, while uncertainties are mostly about limited additive-specific modern long-term epidemiology rather than clear evidence of harm.

### 3. Recommended tier
**Recommended tier: `limited`**.
