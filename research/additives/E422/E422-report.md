# E422 (Glycerol) Research Report

## Identity
- **E-number:** E422
- **Name:** Glycerol (glycerin/glycerine)
- **CAS number(s):** 56-81-5 [5][8]
- **Chemical class:** Trihydric alcohol (polyol), molecular formula C3H8O3 [12]
- **Common synonyms:** 1,2,3-Propanetriol; Glycerin; Glycerine; Trihydroxypropane [5][13]
- **Natural vs synthetic:** Glycerol is endogenous/naturally present as part of triglycerides and phospholipids, and commercial production includes both natural (fat/oil-derived) and synthetic routes (e.g., propene-derived); for this workflow classification: **semi-synthetic** [13]

## Function in Food
- **Mechanism of action:** Glycerol is strongly hygroscopic, so it binds/retains water (humectancy), helps control texture/softness, and functions as a solvent/vehicle for flavors and colors. It also contributes mild sweetness and viscosity effects [13].
- **Common technological roles:** Humectant, solvent/vehicle, nutritive sweetener, texturizer/thickener [13].
- **Common food categories:** Broad food use under GMP in US CFR; PubChem/FDA-linked functional-use entries indicate uses including humectancy and solvent functions in foods (e.g., flavor/color systems and moisture-retaining applications) [4][13].

## Key Safety Evidence

### Animal toxicology and core hazard conclusions
- EFSA 2017 concluded no concern for genotoxicity and carcinogenicity and no need for a numerical ADI in the food-additive context [1].
- JECFA retained ADI “not specified,” consistent with low concern at normal food-use exposure ranges [5].

### Human and mechanistic evidence (recent PubMed)
- **Mechanistic concern signal:** A 2025 human volunteer study reported that gut microbiota can convert dietary glycerol into acrolein, with urinary biomarkers increasing after high-glycerol meals; this is a mechanistic hazard signal rather than proof of disease causation from normal additive exposure [9].
- **Short-term oral intake studies:** A 2026 randomized trial found low-to-moderate glycerol beverage concentrations improved fluid balance in healthy adults; this supports short-term tolerability in controlled settings, but does not establish long-term additive safety at population scale [10].
- **High-dose athletic protocol context:** A 2025 crossover trial (1.2 g/kg glycerol) reported performance/hydration effects without a major acute safety signal in trained runners; doses are much higher than usual dietary additive exposure [11].

### Epidemiological data
- No strong E422-specific long-term epidemiology isolating food-additive glycerol exposure and chronic disease outcomes was identified in the retrieved sources.

## Exposure Assessment
- EFSA 2022 follow-up reports broad estimated exposure ranges:
  - In infants, toddlers, children: from about **1 to 460 mg/kg bw/day** (across scenarios).
  - In adults: from about **2 to 308 mg/kg bw/day**.
  - In refined scenarios, means roughly **2–34 mg/kg bw/day** and P95 roughly **10–170 mg/kg bw/day** across population groups [2].
- ADI exceedance framing is limited because EFSA/JECFA use “ADI not specified.”
- EFSA 2022 nevertheless highlighted residual uncertainty for **toddlers and children** at high-percentile exposure under conservative assumptions [2].

## Risk Assessment

### 1. Tier-by-tier analysis
- **risk_free**
  - Supporting evidence: EFSA and JECFA both effectively support low concern (ADI not specified / no numerical ADI needed); glycerol is an endogenous metabolite and naturally present in lipid biology [1][5][13].
  - Evidence against: EFSA 2022 follow-up could not fully exclude concern for toddlers/children at high-percentile exposure due to uncertainties; recent mechanistic human data suggest possible microbiome-mediated acrolein formation after high-glycerol meals [2][9].

- **limited**
  - Supporting evidence: FDA GRAS (21 CFR 182.1320) with GMP condition; EFSA and JECFA generally permissive conclusions; no IARC classification found; no major bans found [1][3][4][5][7].
  - Evidence against: unresolved uncertainty remains for some high-end pediatric exposure scenarios, and mechanistic concern exists regarding acrolein generation under specific dietary contexts [2][9].

- **moderate**
  - Supporting evidence: EFSA follow-up uncertainty for toddlers/children at high percentiles and mechanistic acrolein evidence could justify caution for specific subgroups/high-intake patterns [2][9].
  - Evidence against: regulators did not set restrictive ADIs or broad prohibitions; evidence does not show robust population-level harm at typical additive exposures [1][3][5].

- **high**
  - Supporting evidence: none identified (no major bans, no IARC Group 2A/2B/1 listing retrieved for glycerol, no regulatory withdrawal). [6][7]
  - Evidence against: major authorities remain permissive under GMP/authorized-use frameworks [1][3][5].

### 2. Rationale
The total evidence profile is broadly permissive, but not completely uncertainty-free. Regulatory bodies (EFSA/JECFA/FDA) support continued use, with no numerical ADI deemed necessary. However, EFSA follow-up highlighted uncertainty at high-end child exposure estimates, and recent microbiome work raises a plausible mechanistic concern (acrolein formation) under high dietary glycerol contexts.

### 3. Recommended tier
**Recommended tier: `limited`**
