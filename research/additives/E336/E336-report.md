# E336 Research Report - Potassium Tartrates (Cream of Tartar)

## Identity
- **E-number:** E336 (potassium tartrates), including potassium acid tartrate (cream of tartar) and related potassium tartrate forms used in EU additive nomenclature. [1][3]
- **Primary confirmed CAS from CFR food-additive listing:** **868-14-4** (potassium acid tartrate). [3]
- **Chemical class:** Tartrate salts of tartaric acid (organic acid salts). [1][5]
- **Common synonyms:** potassium acid tartrate, potassium bitartrate, cream of tartar. [3]
- **Natural vs synthetic:** CFR describes potassium acid tartrate as obtained as a byproduct of wine manufacture; commercial food ingredient is commonly isolated/purified from this stream. [3]

## Function in Food
- **Mechanism / technological role:** Acidulant/pH control and buffering behavior from tartrate chemistry; also used as leavening support and processing aid. [3]
- **CFR functional uses (US):** anticaking, antimicrobial, formulation aid, humectant, leavening agent, pH control agent, processing aid, stabilizer/thickener, surface-active agent. [3]
- **Common food categories (CFR):** baked goods, confections/frostings, gelatins/puddings, hard candy, jams/jellies, soft candy. [3]

## Regulatory Status
### EFSA (EU)
- **Latest EFSA re-evaluation identified:** EFSA Journal 2020 opinion covering tartaric acid/tartrates including **E336**. [1]
- **Status:** approved (re-evaluated; group ADI established). [1]
- **ADI:** **240 mg/kg bw/day** (group ADI, expressed as tartaric acid). [1]
- **Evaluation year:** 2020. [1]
- **Key conclusions:** No genotoxic signal; no carcinogenicity signal in the chronic rat study at the highest tested dose; exposure estimates in EFSA refined non-brand-loyal scenario did not exceed the group ADI. [1]

- No major-jurisdiction ban identified in searched sources.

## Key Safety Evidence
### Animal and toxicology evidence
- EFSA summary reports no toxic effects (including nephrotoxicity) in tested L(+)-form toxicology studies; no indication of genotoxicity; no carcinogenicity signal in chronic rat study at highest tested dose. [1]
- EFSA also reports available maternal/developmental studies without relevant effects, while noting lack of dedicated reproductive toxicity studies. [1]

### Human evidence
- **Case-report toxicity at very high non-food use doses:**
  - 2013 case report: two intentional large cream-of-tartar ingestions with severe hyperkalemia (>8 mmol/L), both recovered after treatment. [10]
  - 2025 case report: hyperkalaemic cardiac arrest after cream-of-tartar ingestion. [9]
- **Dietary/functional human study:** 2003 crossover trial in healthy adults using raisins or 5 g cream of tartar/day found stool-softening and reduced transit time, with no severe acute toxicity reported in abstract. [12]

### Mechanistic concerns
- Main plausible hazard at excess intake is **potassium load -> hyperkalemia**, especially if intake is extreme or non-food-use dosing is attempted. [9][10]
- Veterinary evidence also links tartaric acid-rich exposures to acute kidney injury in dogs (species-specific concern; not directly a human food-use signal). [11]

## Exposure Assessment
- EFSA's refined non-brand-loyal exposure estimates for population groups did **not exceed** the group ADI of 240 mg/kg bw/day. [1]
- Typical food-use exposure therefore appears low relative to EFSA ADI under current assessed uses. [1]
- Exceedance risk is mainly linked to intentional high-dose ingestion outside normal culinary use; this is not a standard dietary pattern. [9][10]
- Potentially vulnerable groups in a precautionary sense: people with impaired potassium handling (e.g., severe kidney disease) if exposed to unusually high supplemental/non-food doses. (Inference from hyperkalemia mechanism and case reports.) [9][10]

## Risk Assessment
### 1. Tier-by-tier analysis
**risk_free**
- Evidence supporting: Naturally associated with food/winemaking streams; broad regulatory approvals; no genotoxic/carcinogenic signal in EFSA summary; EFSA refined exposure below ADI. [1][3]
- Evidence against: Documented life-threatening hyperkalemia after large intentional ingestion; JECFA and EFSA both still use bounded ADIs rather than "ADI not specified". [5][9][10]

**limited**
- Evidence supporting: Approved/GRAS in US; EFSA approved with defined ADI and exposure below ADI; JECFA group ADI maintained; severe effects mainly in overdose/misuse scenarios. [1][3][5][9][10]
- Evidence against: ADI divergence between EFSA (240) and JECFA (0-30) creates some uncertainty margin across frameworks. [1][5]

**moderate**
- Evidence supporting: Credible high-dose toxicity case reports (cardiovascular emergency via hyperkalemia); EU has recently tightened some use conditions for related tartrates. [9][10][13]
- Evidence against: No evidence from EFSA summary that normal dietary exposure exceeds ADI; no major ban; no IARC carcinogenic classification signal found. [1][7][8]

**high**
- Evidence supporting: Severe outcomes can occur in misuse/overdose cases. [9][10]
- Evidence against: Not banned in major jurisdictions in searched sources; no IARC Group 2A/2B/1 signal identified; regulatory bodies continue to permit use with ADIs/GMP controls. [1][3][5][7][8]

### 2. Rationale
Regulatory consensus supports continued use of E336 under food-use conditions (EFSA/JECFA ADIs, FDA GRAS/GMP). The strongest harm signals are acute high-dose misuse case reports, not routine dietary exposure. This pattern aligns with a controlled-risk additive rather than a high-concern carcinogenic or bioaccumulative hazard.

### 3. Recommended tier
**limited**
