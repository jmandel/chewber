# E123 (Amaranth) Research Report

## Identity
- **E-number / INS**: E123 / INS 123. [S4]
- **Primary name**: Amaranth.
- **CAS number**: 915-67-3. [S4]
- **Chemical class**: Synthetic **azo dye** (an anionic trisodium sulfonated azo colorant). [S4, S6]
- **Common synonyms**: FD&C Red No. 2, Acid Red 27, C.I. Food Red 9, Azorubin S, Naphtol Rot S. [S4, S5, S6]
- **Origin**: **Synthetic** (not a natural endogenous nutrient). [S4, S6]

## Function in Food
Amaranth is a water-soluble synthetic red azo color used to impart red/pink hues in foods and beverages. Mechanistically, its azo-conjugated chromophore absorbs visible light and provides color intensity at low concentrations. [S4, S6]

Commonly reported food categories include soft drinks/juice-type drinks and confectionery; EFSA’s EU review context also references use across multiple colored food categories. [S1, S3, S9]

## Regulatory Status
### EFSA (EU)
- **Status**: Evaluated as an already permitted food color (restricted by ADI/use limits rather than withdrawn). [S2, S3]
- **ADI**: EFSA ANS Panel set a **temporary ADI of 0.15 mg/kg bw/day** (reduced from earlier 0.5 mg/kg bw/day) in its re-evaluation. [S1, S2]
- **Evaluation year**: Re-evaluation in **2010**; refined exposure update in **2013**. [S2, S3]
- **Key conclusions**:
  - Not considered carcinogenic or genotoxic at assessed evidence level. [S1]
  - Under conservative maximum-permitted-use assumptions, ADI exceedance was possible (especially children). [S2, S3]
  - In refined (reported-use) scenarios, exposure was generally below ADI, with some high-percentile child estimates still above ADI in specific surveys. [S3]

### FDA / CFR (United States)
- eCFR search for **FD&C Red No. 2** maps to **21 CFR Part 81** (not current positive food-color listings for use in foods). [S7]
- **21 CFR 81.10(f)**: termination of the provisional listing of FD&C Red No. 2 for food/drug/cosmetic use. [S8]
- **21 CFR 81.30(j)(1)**: certificates for FD&C Red No. 2 canceled; further use after the specified date renders products adulterated. [S10]
- **GRAS status**: Not GRAS for food use; effectively **not permitted/banned for food use** via Part 81 termination/cancellation framework. [S7, S8, S10]

### JECFA / WHO
- WHO/JECFA database entry for AMARANTH (ID 996) reports:
  - **ADI**: **0-0.5 mg/kg bw**
  - **Latest listed evaluation year**: **1984** (meeting 28). [S4]

### IARC
- Checked IARC Monographs classification page and site search endpoints for “Amaranth”, “FD&C Red No. 2”, “Food Red 9”: no individual listing located for this additive term set in the consulted pages. [S11, S12]
- Practical interpretation: **no identified IARC group classification found for Amaranth in the queried IARC resources**.

### Notable bans / restrictions
- **United States**: FD&C Red No. 2 food-use status terminated/certificates canceled under 21 CFR Part 81. [S8, S10]

## Key Safety Evidence
### Animal and mechanistic studies
- A 2025 zebrafish model study reported dose-dependent developmental/neurobehavioral toxicity signals, oxidative stress, and alpha-synuclein-related changes at tested concentrations; authors called for reassessment of limits. [S13]
- A 2020 zebrafish embryo study on four azo dyes (including amaranth) found developmental toxicity at tested high concentrations; authors also noted these concentrations were above expected human dietary exposures. [S15]

### Human exposure / population-risk evidence
- A 2022 Brazilian exposure assessment found population mean intake below ADI, but “consumers-only” scenarios and younger groups (especially adolescents) could exceed ADI, including worst-case estimates up to ~3x ADI. [S14]

### Regulatory toxicology context
- EFSA 2010 did not classify amaranth as genotoxic/carcinogenic in the reviewed evidence but lowered ADI and highlighted exposure uncertainty/exceedance concerns in conservative scenarios. [S1, S2]

## Exposure Assessment
- **EFSA conservative (MPL-based) scenario**: mean and high-percentile estimates could exceed the ADI, particularly in children (reported ranges up to several-fold ADI in some scenarios). [S2, S3]
- **EFSA refined/reported-use scenario (2013)**: exposures mostly below ADI, but high-percentile children in at least one survey remained above ADI. [S3]
- **Recent country-level evidence (Brazil)**: younger high consumers of colored beverages appear most at risk of exceedance. [S14]

### Vulnerable populations
- Children/adolescents with frequent intake of brightly colored drinks/confectionery are the most plausible ADI-exceedance group. [S3, S9, S14]

## Risk Assessment
### 1. Tier-by-tier analysis
#### risk_free
- **Evidence supporting**:
  - EFSA did not find carcinogenic/genotoxic concern in its re-evaluation dataset. [S1]
  - JECFA and EFSA both provide ADI-based frameworks. [S2, S4]
- **Evidence against**:
  - ADI exceedance is plausible in some child/high-consumer scenarios. [S2, S3, S14]
  - U.S. food-use status was terminated historically under CFR Part 81. [S8, S10]
  - Recent animal/mechanistic studies report neurodevelopmental signals. [S13, S15]

#### limited
- **Evidence supporting**:
  - Regulatory bodies (EFSA/JECFA) retain ADI-based management rather than universal prohibition. [S2, S3, S4]
  - Refined EFSA exposure was largely below ADI. [S3]
- **Evidence against**:
  - Multiple datasets identify subgroups (children/adolescents/high consumers) with exceedance risk. [S3, S14]
  - U.S. regulatory endpoint is non-permitted for food use. [S8, S10]

#### moderate
- **Evidence supporting**:
  - Matches definition of approved-with-caveats: ADI reduced by EFSA; exceedance risk in some populations; non-clinical toxicity signals at higher doses. [S2, S3, S13, S14, S15]
- **Evidence against**:
  - Core carcinogenic/genotoxic concern was not confirmed by EFSA review; refined exposure often below ADI. [S1, S3]

#### high
- **Evidence supporting**:
  - Major-jurisdiction historical prohibition/non-permitted status in U.S. food use. [S8, S10]
- **Evidence against**:
  - Not accompanied by a current IARC Group 1/2 classification in checked resources; EFSA/JECFA still use ADI-based control rather than global withdrawal. [S1, S3, S4, S11, S12]

### 2. Rationale
The strongest drivers are: (a) established ADIs with mixed exposure outcomes, (b) repeated indication that high-consuming children/adolescents can exceed ADI in specific scenarios, and (c) persistent but mainly non-human mechanistic toxicity signals. This is more severe than “limited,” but does not clearly meet “high” across major regulators globally.

### 3. Recommended tier
**Recommended tier: `moderate`**
