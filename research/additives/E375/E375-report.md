# E375 (Niacin / Vitamin B3) — Research Report

## Identity
- **E-number:** E375 (historical/legacy E-number attribution to niacin/nicotinic acid).
- **Primary chemical name:** Niacin (nicotinic acid; pyridine-3-carboxylic acid).
- **CAS number(s):**
  - **59-67-6** (niacin / nicotinic acid).
  - Related B3 vitamer in regulation: **98-92-0** (niacinamide; distinct CFR entry).
- **Chemical class:** Water-soluble B-complex vitamin (pyridinecarboxylic acid).
- **Common synonyms:** Niacin, Vitamin B3, Nicotinic acid, Pyridine-3-carboxylic acid.
- **Natural vs synthetic:** Both exist; naturally present in foods, but additive/fortification use is typically manufactured (synthetic) food-grade niacin.

## Function in Food
- **Primary technological role:** Nutrient supplement/fortificant.
- **Mechanism of action:** Provides vitamin B3 activity via conversion to NAD/NADP coenzyme systems involved in cellular redox and metabolism.
- **Common food categories:** Enriched/fortified grain products, cereals, breads, and infant formula (where permitted under specific legal frameworks).

## Key Safety Evidence
- **Human high-dose adverse effects signal (strongest available safety signal):**
  - NIH ODS synthesis and recent review evidence indicate oral niacin/nicotinamide adverse effects are dose-dependent, with flushing common at supplemental nicotinic acid doses and hepatotoxicity risk at pharmacologic/high chronic doses.
  - Extended-release/high-dose therapeutic niacin datasets report higher adverse-event burden in treated groups (e.g., metabolic, GI, hepatic, skin-related concerns) without clear cardiovascular outcome benefit in large modern adjunct trials.
- **Recent review evidence:**
  - 2025 overview-of-reviews (`PMID: 40999226`) compiles adverse-effect evidence for oral niacin/nicotinamide.
  - 2017 meta-analysis (`PMID: 28541582`) addresses tolerable upper intake definition and dose-response adverse effects.
- **Animal/mechanistic context:**
  - Mechanistic plausibility for vasodilatory flushing and dose-related toxicity at pharmacologic exposure is established in regulatory/clinical literature synthesis; however, recent food-additive-level chronic toxicology specifically isolating E375 from supplement/pharmacologic contexts is limited.
- **Epidemiological/clinical context:**
  - Available modern concern signal is driven primarily by therapeutic-dose clinical evidence rather than typical food-fortification exposure.

## Exposure Assessment
- **Typical intake levels (U.S. population, foods/beverages):**
  - NHANES 2015–2016 summary on NIH ODS page: approximately **21.4 mg/day** (ages 2–19), **31.4 mg/day** (adult men), **21.3 mg/day** (adult women).
- **Upper level context:**
  - NIH ODS cites FNB UL framework for supplemental niacin; adult UL commonly represented as **35 mg/day** for supplemental forms.
- **ADI exceedance risk (food additive context):**
  - No numeric EFSA/JECFA ADI for E375 was retrieved.
  - Practical exceedance concern is primarily for **high-dose supplement/therapeutic use**, not typical dietary fortification intake.
- **Potentially vulnerable populations:**
  - People using high-dose niacin supplements or therapeutic niacin regimens.
  - Individuals with pre-existing liver disease, glucose intolerance/metabolic risk, or interacting medical conditions requiring monitoring.

## Risk Assessment
### 1. Tier-by-tier analysis
#### risk_free
- **Evidence supporting risk_free:**
  - Niacin is an essential nutrient and naturally present in foods.
  - FDA permits niacin as GRAS nutrient supplement at GMP.
- **Evidence arguing against risk_free:**
  - Clear dose-related adverse effects are documented at supplemental/pharmacologic doses (flushing, hepatic and metabolic concerns).
  - No directly retrievable EFSA food-additive ADI/re-evaluation result for E375 in accessed EFSA endpoints.

#### limited
- **Evidence supporting limited:**
  - FDA GRAS status with GMP conditions and established nutrient-use framework.
  - No IARC carcinogenic classification for niacin identified.
  - Main serious harms are linked to high supplemental/therapeutic dosing rather than ordinary food-use exposure.
- **Evidence arguing against limited:**
  - Regulatory evidence for EU food-additive-specific E375 evaluation was incomplete from accessible EFSA/JECFA endpoints.

#### moderate
- **Evidence supporting moderate:**
  - Robust high-dose human adverse-event evidence exists.
  - Supplemental intakes can exceed UL and require clinical monitoring in some populations.
- **Evidence arguing against moderate:**
  - This concern profile is primarily pharmacologic-dose dependent; it does not map cleanly to normal food additive/fortification exposure.
  - No broad regulatory ban signal was found.

#### high
- **Evidence supporting high:**
  - None from reviewed authoritative sources.
- **Evidence arguing against high:**
  - FDA GRAS affirmation remains in force.
  - No IARC Group 1/2A/2B listing for niacin identified.
  - No major-jurisdiction ban identified.

### 2. Rationale
The hazard profile is dose-dependent: niacin has well-documented adverse effects at high supplemental/therapeutic doses, but ordinary food-use/fortification exposure does not show equivalent harm signals. The strongest regulatory signal is FDA GRAS with GMP conditions. EFSA food-additive-specific data for E375 were not directly retrievable from accessible EFSA endpoints, which lowers certainty but does not itself indicate high risk.

### 3. Recommended tier
**limited**
