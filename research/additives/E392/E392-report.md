# E392 (Rosemary extract) — Research Report

## Identity
- **E-number / INS number:** E392 / INS 392.
- **Primary name:** Rosemary extract (extract of *Rosmarinus officinalis* L.; now *Salvia rosmarinus*).
- **CAS number(s):**
  - **84604-14-8** (extract of rosemary)
  - **3650-09-7** (carnosic acid)
  - **5957-80-2** (carnosol)
- **Chemical class:** Botanical antioxidant extract rich in **phenolic diterpenes** (mainly carnosic acid and carnosol).
- **Common synonyms:** Extract of rosemary, rosemary antioxidant extract, rosemary oleoresin/extracts.
- **Natural vs synthetic:** **Natural** (plant-derived extract), although commercial products are standardized during manufacturing.

## Function in Food
- **Primary technological role:** Antioxidant.
- **Mechanism of action:** Antioxidative activity is mainly from **carnosic acid and carnosol**, which inhibit oxidation (especially lipid oxidation) and help preserve fats/oils-containing foods.
- **Common food categories (from EFSA/JECFA exposure contexts):** fine bakery wares, processed nuts, soups and broths, sauces, meat products, and fat-containing processed foods.

## Regulatory Status
### EFSA (EU)
- **Approval status:** **Approved/authorised** as food additive E392 in the EU.
- **Latest EFSA scientific output identified:** **2018** refined exposure assessment (`EFSA Journal 2018;16(8):5373`).
- **ADI (EFSA):** EFSA reports for rosemary extracts noted insufficient data to set a numerical EFSA ADI in earlier assessments; the 2018 refined exposure opinion benchmarked exposure against the **JECFA temporary ADI 0–0.3 mg/kg bw/day** used at that time.
- **Key conclusion:** EFSA’s refined exposure assessment stated that the estimates were likely conservative and that use of rosemary extracts (E392) as currently reported did **not raise a safety concern**.
- **Access note:** Direct EFSA journal full-text endpoints were Cloudflare-protected in this VM; data were extracted from the EFSA records and indexed abstracts for the EFSA URLs listed in Sources.

### FDA / CFR (United States)
- **Status:** **GRAS** framework in Title 21 for rosemary in spice/extractive categories.
- **CFR citations:**
  - **21 CFR 182.10** (Spices and other natural seasonings and flavorings) — includes rosemary.
  - **21 CFR 182.20** (Essential oils, oleoresins (solvent-free), and natural extractives (including distillates)) — includes rosemary.
- **Conditions of use:** Listed as generally recognized as safe for intended use (section 409 context); 182.20 specifically addresses solvent-free oleoresins/natural extractives.
- **Access note:** Human-facing eCFR pages were CAPTCHA-protected from this environment; authoritative text was extracted via official eCFR API XML.

### JECFA / WHO
- **Latest evaluation year:** **2025** (JECFA 100).
- **Current ADI:** **0–0.6 mg/kg bw/day** (expressed as carnosic acid and carnosol).
- **Prior ADI history:** Temporary ADI **0–0.3 mg/kg bw/day** in 2016 and retained in 2019; then withdrawn and replaced in 2025.
- **Key 2025 conclusion:** Based on new developmental data, JECFA withdrew the temporary ADI and set 0–0.6 mg/kg bw/day; high child exposure scenarios could slightly exceed ADI, but JECFA did not consider this a safety concern given MOE and conservative assumptions.

### IARC
- **Classification status for rosemary extract/E392:** **No IARC monograph classification identified**.
- Verification was done against IARC’s classifications pages and the IARC classification dataset script (no rosemary/carnosic/carnosol entries found).

### Notable bans
- No major-jurisdiction ban was identified from the EFSA/FDA/JECFA/IARC sources reviewed.

## Key Safety Evidence
- **Animal/developmental evidence (regulatory critical study line):**
  - JECFA 2025 cites rat-pup thyroid hormone findings after maternal dietary exposure, with **BMDL20 = 97 mg/kg bw/day** (as carnosic acid + carnosol), above the previous NOAEL basis (64 mg/kg bw/day), supporting ADI revision to 0–0.6.
- **Animal reproductive/developmental toxicology (recent PubMed study):**
  - Phipps et al. (2021) reproductive/developmental screening of an acetone rosemary extract reported no treatment-related reproductive or developmental toxicity at tested levels (NOAEL 563.6 mg/kg bw/day for tested extract).
- **Human safety evidence (supplement context, not additive-intake context):**
  - Kuwata et al. (2025) open-label and randomized crossover study reported no clinically relevant adverse findings in the safety phase; stress outcomes were exploratory efficacy endpoints.
  - Draelos et al. (2025) randomized placebo-controlled supplement trial reported generally good tolerability; one participant discontinued with moderate GI upset.
- **Mechanistic considerations:**
  - JECFA 2025 report discusses enzyme/transporter and biotransformation findings (including CYP-related effects) as mechanistic context; current regulatory ADI already incorporates toxicological uncertainty handling.
- **Epidemiological data:**
  - No robust additive-specific human epidemiology directly isolating E392 intake was identified in the searched PubMed set.

## Exposure Assessment
- **EFSA refined exposure (2018):** mean exposure approximately **0.09–0.38 mg/kg bw/day**, with 95th percentile approximately **0.14–0.45 mg/kg bw/day** (highest in toddlers in EFSA scenarios).
- **JECFA 2025 high dietary exposure scenario:** up to **0.84 mg/kg bw/day in children** and **0.32 mg/kg bw/day in adults** (combined naturally occurring + added sources at typical use levels).
- **ADI exceedance risk:**
  - Under the current JECFA ADI (0–0.6), high-end child scenarios can exceed the upper bound; adults in cited scenario were below it.
  - JECFA judged the slight child exceedance not to indicate a safety concern in the assessed conservative scenario.
- **Potentially vulnerable populations:** high-consuming children and populations with high cumulative intake of foods using rosemary extract.

## Risk Assessment
### 1. Tier-by-tier analysis
#### risk_free
- **Evidence supporting risk_free:** natural plant-derived extract; no IARC carcinogenic classification; authorised in major jurisdictions.
- **Evidence against risk_free:** numerical ADI exists (not “ADI not specified”); high-end child exposure can exceed ADI in conservative scenarios; developmental thyroid-hormone signal is part of risk characterization.

#### limited
- **Evidence supporting limited:** approved/GRAS frameworks are in place (EU authorised; U.S. GRAS listings in CFR); JECFA finalized ADI with updated toxicology.
- **Evidence against limited:** repeated regulatory attention to developmental endpoints and child high-end exposure exceedance indicates meaningful caveats beyond a purely low-concern profile.

#### moderate
- **Evidence supporting moderate:** approved but with caveats: child high-end intake can exceed ADI, and credible animal developmental thyroid findings were central to ADI revisions.
- **Evidence against moderate:** JECFA and EFSA conclusions did not identify a current safety concern at assessed use assumptions.

#### high
- **Evidence supporting high:** no strong support found (no major-jurisdiction ban, no IARC Group 1/2A/2B assignment for rosemary extract).
- **Evidence against high:** current approvals and updated ADI remain in force; regulators did not conclude unsafe use under assessed conditions.

### 2. Rationale
The weight of evidence indicates regulatory acceptance with explicit caveats rather than unrestricted low concern: a clear ADI is in place, developmental thyroid findings were considered relevant, and high-end child intake may exceed ADI in conservative scenarios. At the same time, major authorities did not conclude that current uses are unsafe.

### 3. Recommended tier
**moderate**
