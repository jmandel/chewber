# E385 (Calcium disodium EDTA) Research Report

## Identity
- **E-number:** E385
- **Primary name:** Calcium disodium EDTA (calcium disodium ethylenediaminetetraacetate)
- **CAS number(s):** `662-33-9` (as listed on WHO/JECFA chemical page for INS 385)
- **Chemical class:** Aminopolycarboxylate chelating agent (EDTA salt)
- **Common synonyms:** Calcium disodium EDTA; calcium disodium edetate; calcium disodium (ethylene-dinitrilo)-tetraacetate
- **Natural vs synthetic:** Synthetic

## Function in Food
- **Primary technological role:** Sequestrant (chelating agent)
- **Mechanism:** Binds polyvalent metal ions (e.g., Fe, Cu), reducing metal-catalyzed oxidation and quality loss.
- **Common food categories (regulatory examples):** pickled vegetables, canned soft drinks, canned seafood, mayonnaise/salad dressings/sauces, canned legumes, fermented malt beverages, distilled spirits.
- **U.S. technical role examples in regulation:** preservative, color/flavor retention aid, antigushing agent, anti-struvite aid in canned seafood.

## Regulatory Status
### EFSA (EU)
- **Current status:** Authorised food additive under re-evaluation workflow.
- **Latest scientific opinion identified:** EFSA Journal opinion page for ferric sodium EDTA (2018) states there was **no scientific justification to increase the current ADI** and references the current EDTA ADI basis from calcium disodium EDTA data.
- **ADI used by EFSA in that opinion:** **1.9 mg EDTA/kg bw/day**, equivalent to **2.5 mg calcium disodium EDTA/kg bw/day**.
- **Latest re-evaluation process signal:** EFSA call-for-data page (archived) indicates the reassessment timeline anticipated completion by end of 2026.
- **Key conclusion extracted:** Maintain current ADI; exposure concerns are mainly for high-intake infant scenarios in the specific ferric sodium EDTA use assessment context.

### FDA / eCFR (U.S.)
- **Status:** Approved food additive (not GRAS-listed in Parts 182/184/186 based on API checks).
- **CFR citation:** **21 CFR 172.120**.
- **Conditions of use:** “May be safely used” in designated foods with explicit ppm ceilings by category and labeling/use-direction requirements; combined limits also specified with disodium EDTA in certain foods.

### JECFA / WHO
- **Chemical record:** WHO JECFA chemical page for calcium disodium ethylenediaminetetraacetate (INS 385).
- **ADI:** **0-2.5 mg/kg bw**.
- **Evaluation year shown:** **1973** (with earlier/related historical entries listed in previous years).
- **Comment shown:** ADI expressed as calcium disodium EDTA, with note that no excess disodium EDTA should remain in foods.

### IARC
- **Classification result:** No EDTA/calcium disodium EDTA entry found in the current IARC “Agents classified” dataset endpoint (last update string in source: 2026-02-25).

### Notable bans
- No major-jurisdiction outright ban identified in the consulted sources.

## Key Safety Evidence
### Animal/toxicological evidence
- WHO/INCHEM monograph indicates **low oral absorption** (roughly low single-digit % in humans/animals in older studies) and high fecal excretion.
- Older toxicology summaries report **renal toxicity mainly at high parenteral doses**; long-term dietary studies in rats/dogs at tested dietary concentrations did not show strong chronic toxicity signals driving ADI removal.
- JECFA retained numeric ADI (0-2.5 mg/kg bw), not “ADI not specified”.

### Human/exposure-oriented evidence
- PubMed 2026 beverage survey/risk assessment detected calcium disodium EDTA in sampled beverages and found cumulative hazard index values below 1, with **higher relative exposure in children** due to lower body weight.
- Recent PubMed items include non-food contexts (cosmetic safety panel update; cadmium-chelation animal model), useful for mechanistic context but limited for direct dietary causal inference.

### Mechanistic concerns
- As a chelator, EDTA can alter metal availability; concern is mostly dose/context dependent and tied to high exposure scenarios rather than routine compliant use levels.

## Exposure Assessment
- **Regulatory benchmark:** ADI 1.9 mg/kg bw/day (EFSA context as EDTA; equivalent to 2.5 mg/kg bw/day as calcium disodium EDTA) and JECFA ADI 0-2.5 mg/kg bw/day.
- **Exceedance risk:**
  - EFSA 2018 ferric sodium EDTA assessment context flagged potential exceedance at high intake for some infants in scenario modeling.
  - The 2026 beverage study indicates child exposure is relatively higher, though aggregate HI remained below 1 in that dataset.
- **Vulnerable populations:** Infants and children with high intake patterns of preserved foods/beverages; populations with unusual cumulative EDTA exposure from multiple sources.

## Risk Assessment
### 1) Tier-by-tier analysis
**risk_free**
- Evidence supporting: Regulatory approvals exist; low oral absorption is documented.
- Evidence against: Numeric ADIs are set (not “ADI not specified”); potential high-intake exceedance scenarios are documented.

**limited**
- Evidence supporting: FDA-approved with specific limits; EFSA/JECFA maintain ADI-based frameworks; no IARC carcinogenic listing identified.
- Evidence against: Exposure caveats for infants/high consumers and high-dose renal effects in legacy toxicology reduce confidence in a purely low-concern classification.

**moderate**
- Evidence supporting: Established ADI with explicit exposure-management caveats; infant high-intake scenario concerns in EFSA context; chelation-related biological plausibility and legacy high-dose renal findings.
- Evidence against: No strong contemporary evidence of serious harm at compliant dietary levels; many data points remain conservative/risk-management based rather than clear harm signals.

**high**
- Evidence supporting: High-dose toxicity exists in non-dietary contexts.
- Evidence against: No major-jurisdiction ban identified, no IARC Group 2A/2B/1 listing for EDTA in consulted classification dataset, and no regulator conclusion that safety cannot be established at authorized-use conditions.

### 2) Rationale
E385 sits between routine approval and meaningful caveats: it is regulated with explicit use limits and ADI control, but not treated as concern-free. The strongest reasons against a lower tier are infant/high-intake exceedance scenarios and longstanding high-dose toxicity signals that justify conservative intake caps.

### 3) Recommended tier
- **Recommended tier: `moderate`**
