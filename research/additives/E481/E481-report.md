# E481 (Sodium stearoyl-2-lactylate) — Research Report

## Identity
- **E-number:** E481.
- **Name:** Sodium stearoyl-2-lactylate (often written as sodium stearoyl lactylate; SSL).
- **CAS numbers:** `25383-99-7` (listed in CFR as CAS Reg. No. 25-383-997 formatting), and `5793-94-2` (stearoyl-2-lactylic acid, related parent acid in toxicology/indexing records).
  - Sources: eCFR §172.846 API text; PubMed chemical indexing for stearoyl-2-lactylic acid.
- **Chemical class:** Lactylate ester salt / anionic emulsifier used as a dough strengthener and processing aid.
  - Source: eCFR §172.846.
- **Common synonyms:** Sodium stearoyl lactylate; sodium salts of stearoyl lactylic acids; SSL.
  - Source: eCFR §172.846 and PubMed naming.
- **Natural vs synthetic:** **Synthetic (industrial synthesis)**, produced by reacting stearic acid with lactic acid, then converting to sodium salts.
  - Source: eCFR §172.846(a).

## Function in Food
- **Primary role:** Emulsifier/dough strengthener/processing aid in bakery systems.
  - Source: eCFR §172.846(c)(1).
- **Mechanism of action (functional):** Improves dispersion at fat-water and dough interfaces and is used to stabilize/emulsify food systems (including bakery matrices).
  - Sources: eCFR functional uses; Food Chemistry study on SSL-based lamellar systems in bread applications.
- **Common food categories (U.S. listed uses):**
  - Baked products, pancakes, waffles (up to 0.5 parts per 100 parts flour)
  - Icings/fillings/puddings/toppings (up to 0.2%)
  - Edible fat-water emulsions for coffee whiteners (up to 0.3%)
  - Dehydrated potatoes (up to 0.5% dry weight)
  - Snack dips (up to 0.2%)
  - Cheese substitutes/imitations (up to 0.2%)
  - Sauces/gravies and foods containing them (up to 0.25%)
  - Cream liqueur drinks (up to 0.5%)
  - Source: eCFR §172.846(c)(1)-(9).

## Regulatory Status
- **EFSA (EU):** Re-evaluated E481/E482 in 2013; established a **group ADI of 22 mg/kg bw/day** (expressed as lactic acid) for stearoyl-2-lactylates (E481 and E482). EFSA concluded that mean and high-level exposure estimates for reported uses did not indicate a safety concern.
  - Source: EFSA Journal opinion page (2013) for E481/E482 re-evaluation.
- **FDA / CFR (U.S.):** Regulated as an **approved direct food additive** under **21 CFR 172.846** with explicit use conditions and maximum levels by food category.
  - **GRAS status:** No specific Part 184 GRAS listing for sodium stearoyl lactylate was found in current eCFR part text; regulatory basis is 21 CFR 172.846 food additive permission.
  - Sources: eCFR §172.846 API text; eCFR Part 184 API text scan.
- **JECFA/WHO:** Group ADI reported as **0-20 mg/kg bw** for sodium stearoyl-2-lactylate; evaluation year shown as **1973** in the WHO JECFA record (with later specification-history updates).
  - Sources: WHO JECFA database record (`chemID=3446`) and FAO JECFA additive detail page.
- **IARC:** No specific IARC monograph classification entry identified for sodium stearoyl-2-lactylate in the current IARC classification dataset.
  - Sources: IARC list-of-classifications page + IARC app dataset (`loc.app.js`, last_update field and agent list search).
- **Notable bans/restrictions:** No major-jurisdiction outright ban identified in reviewed EFSA/FDA/JECFA/IARC sources.

## Key Safety Evidence
- **Animal toxicity:** A one-year rat oral toxicity study reported no toxicologically significant adverse findings at tested dietary levels; NOAEL was placed at the highest dose (5% diet; ~2214 mg/kg/day males, ~2641 mg/kg/day females).
  - Source: Food and Chemical Toxicology (2010), PubMed 20600527.
- **Gut microbiome mechanistic signal (in vitro):** Human fecal microbiota culture study reported that 0.025% SSL altered community structure, reduced butyrate-associated taxa, reduced butyrate, and increased pro-inflammatory markers (LPS/flagellin).
  - Source: Frontiers in Microbiology (2020), PubMed 32499766.
- **Population exposure modeling:** Belgian exposure analysis found that conservative MPL-based scenarios could exceed ADI in many children, but refined concentration-based scenarios were mostly below ADI; ~1.9% of children still exceeded ADI.
  - Source: Food Additives & Contaminants Part A (2018), PubMed 29448899.
- **Human hypersensitivity signal:** Published case report of allergic contact dermatitis from sodium stearoyl lactylate exposure.
  - Source: Contact Dermatitis (2005), PubMed 16033408.
- **Recent literature context:** A 2025 industrial bread composition paper identifies SSL as a common bread emulsifier and discusses potential IBD relevance at a food-pattern/additive context level, but does not establish direct causal human toxicity for E481.
  - Source: Nutrients (2025), PubMed 40647225.

## Exposure Assessment
- **Regulatory exposure framing:** EFSA’s 2013 re-evaluation did not identify safety concern for mean/high consumers at then-reported use levels (group ADI 22 mg/kg bw/day).
- **Real-world intake variability:** Belgian data indicate that exceedance risk is highly scenario-dependent (MPL assumptions vs measured market levels), with children the most sensitive subgroup.
- **Likely vulnerable populations:**
  - Children with high bakery-product consumption.
  - Individuals with very high intake of ultra-processed foods containing multiple emulsifiers.
  - People with pre-existing gut inflammatory conditions (hypothesis-generating concern from mechanistic/emulsifier literature, not definitive E481 clinical proof).

## Risk Assessment
### 1. Tier-by-tier analysis
- **risk_free**
  - Evidence for: Long history of regulatory approvals; high-dose animal study without clear adverse effect at tested levels.
  - Evidence against: Regulators set finite ADIs (not “ADI not specified”); mechanistic microbiome concern exists; child exceedance can occur in some intake scenarios.
- **limited**
  - Evidence for: EFSA/JECFA ADIs established; U.S. CFR approval with explicit concentration limits; no IARC listing; no major bans found.
  - Evidence against: In vitro microbiome-inflammatory signals and limited hypersensitivity case reports argue this is not completely concern-free.
- **moderate**
  - Evidence for: Credible mechanistic signal (microbiome/butyrate effects) and some child ADI exceedance in refined population analysis.
  - Evidence against: Current human evidence for clinically significant harm from E481 at authorized levels is limited; major regulators continue to permit use.
- **high**
  - Evidence for: None from reviewed EFSA/FDA/JECFA/IARC sources.
  - Evidence against: No major-jurisdiction ban, no IARC Group 2A/2B listing for this additive, and no regulatory finding of inability to establish safe use conditions.

### 2. Rationale
Weight of evidence supports a permitted additive with established ADI limits and mostly reassuring regulatory toxicology, while non-clinical microbiome findings and occasional exposure exceedance in children justify caution rather than “risk-free” classification.

### 3. Recommended tier
**limited**
