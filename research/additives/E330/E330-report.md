# E330 (Citric acid) Research Report

## Identity
- **E-number:** E330
- **Name:** Citric acid
- **CAS numbers:** 77-92-9 (anhydrous), 5949-29-1 (monohydrate) (Source [6])
- **Chemical class:** Alpha-hydroxy tricarboxylic acid (2-hydroxy-1,2,3-propanetricarboxylic acid) (Sources [4], [6])
- **Common synonyms:** 2-hydroxy-1,2,3-propane-tricarboxylic acid; citrate (salt form context) (Sources [4], [6])
- **Natural vs synthetic:** Naturally occurring in plant/animal tissues; commercial material can be extracted from fruit juices or produced by microbial fermentation (e.g., *Candida spp.*, *Aspergillus niger*) (Source [4])

## Function in Food
- **Primary technological role:** Acidulant / pH control.
- **Secondary roles:** Sequestrant/chelating support, antioxidant synergist, flavouring support roles in formulations (Source [6]).
- **Mechanism:** Lowers pH and buffers acidity; can chelate metal ions, which can support flavor stability and reduce oxidation catalysis in some systems.
- **Common food categories:** Broad use under GMP/quantum satis style conditions in the US GRAS listing (no specific upper limit in 21 CFR 184.1033 beyond GMP) (Source [4]).

## Key Safety Evidence
### Animal / experimental evidence
- EFSA 2020 (E472a-f context): no adverse effects relevant for humans identified for E472a-f dataset; no numerical ADI needed for E472a,b,c; exposure scenarios not indicating safety concern at reported uses (Source [8]).
- EFSA 2020 detailed text reports kidney calcification in short/subchronic E472c testing only at very high doses (>30,000 mg/kg bw/day equivalent), considered not relevant by panel due excessive concentration context (Source [8]).
- Mouse mechanistic studies reported potential metabolic and gut-barrier effects under enhanced citrate feeding:
  - 2021: acute insulin resistance/hyperinsulinemia markers (Source [13]).
  - 2025: increased colon permeability/inflammation and microbiota shifts in a high-citrate feeding model (Source [14]).

### Epidemiological / human data
- No strong human epidemiology isolating **E330 alone** was identified.
- A large cohort analysis reported associations between **food additive mixtures** (one mixture included citric acid/sodium citrates among many additives) and type 2 diabetes incidence; this is not substance-specific causation for E330 (Source [15]).

### Mechanistic / local tolerance concerns
- Multiple recent in vitro dental studies show citric-acid solutions can increase enamel/dentin erosion risk, especially with lower pH and/or higher concentration (Sources [10], [11], [12]).
- These findings are local exposure effects (oral surface demineralization), not equivalent to systemic toxicity at typical additive intake.

## Exposure Assessment
- **Regulatory toxicology benchmark context:** JECFA ADI is "NOT LIMITED" (Source [6]); CFR allows GMP-limited use (Source [4]).
- **Typical dietary intake:** No harmonized, current numeric intake estimate for E330 alone was located in the sources retrieved here.
- **ADI exceedance risk:** For a "not limited" ADI framework, classical numeric ADI exceedance is generally not the main risk discriminator.
- **Potentially vulnerable groups:**
  - Individuals with high acidic-beverage exposure and pre-existing erosion risk (dental sensitivity/erosion context) (Sources [10], [11], [12]).
  - Infant-specific additive risk questions were addressed for related E472c use by EFSA 2025 with no safety concern at assessed levels (Source [3]).

## Risk Assessment
### 1. Tier-by-tier analysis
- **risk_free**
  - Evidence supporting: Endogenous/naturally occurring molecule; JECFA ADI "NOT LIMITED"; US GRAS affirmation with GMP use; no IARC standalone carcinogenic classification identified (Sources [4], [6], [7], [9]).
  - Evidence against: Recent animal/mechanistic papers report possible metabolic/gut effects under enhanced citrate feeding models; local erosive effects in acidic oral exposures are reproducible (Sources [10]-[14]).

- **limited**
  - Evidence supporting: Broad regulatory acceptance (US GRAS, JECFA not-limited ADI), no major regulatory carcinogenic signal for E330, and concerns are mainly at high/localized acidic exposure or non-definitive mechanistic models (Sources [4], [6], [10]-[15]).
  - Evidence against: Some newer experimental studies raise hypotheses that deserve follow-up, which prevents a stronger "risk_free" confidence call (Sources [13], [14]).

- **moderate**
  - Evidence supporting: Presence of credible recent mechanistic signals (metabolic/gut findings in mice) and consistent local dental erosion risk in acidic conditions (Sources [10]-[14]).
  - Evidence against: No regulatory downgrading, no numeric ADI tightening for E330 from JECFA, no IARC carcinogenic class assignment for citric acid, and no robust E330-specific human causal epidemiology (Sources [6], [7], [9], [15]).

- **high**
  - Evidence supporting: Very limited; there is no major-jurisdiction ban and no IARC 2A/2B/1 classification for citric acid itself from checked sources.
  - Evidence against: Regulatory bodies maintain permissive status; JECFA ADI remains "NOT LIMITED"; available concerns are not at a level consistent with a high-risk classification (Sources [4], [6], [7], [9]).

### 2. Rationale
The strongest evidence base remains regulatory-toxicological: JECFA "NOT LIMITED" and FDA GRAS/GMP use. Signals of concern exist, but they are mainly (a) local acidity-driven erosion effects and (b) emerging mechanistic animal findings not yet converted into consistent regulatory restrictions or E330-specific human causal evidence.

### 3. Recommended tier
**Recommended tier: `limited`**
