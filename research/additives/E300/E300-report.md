# E300 Research Report — Ascorbic acid (Vitamin C)

## Identity
- **E-number:** E300 (INS 300)
- **Primary name:** Ascorbic acid (L-ascorbic acid)
- **CAS number:** 50-81-7
- **Chemical class:** Water-soluble vitamin (vitamin C), lactone/organic acid with antioxidant reducing activity
- **Common synonyms:** Vitamin C; L-Ascorbic acid; 3-Keto-L-gulofuranolactone; 2,3-Dehydro-L-threo-hexono-1,4-lactone
- **Natural vs synthetic:** Naturally present in foods (fruit/vegetables) and also manufactured industrially for additive use; manufactured and natural forms are the same active molecule.

## Function in Food
- **Primary technological role:** Antioxidant (JECFA functional class: “Food Additives / ANTIOXIDANT”).
- **Mechanism of action:** Reducing agent that donates electrons, helping limit oxidative reactions (e.g., browning/oxidative quality loss) and supporting stability in processed foods.
- **Common food categories:** Widely used in processed foods where oxidation control is needed, including beverages, fruit/vegetable products, and processed formulations requiring antioxidant protection; in U.S. GRAS listing, use is under GMP rather than fixed maximum for this section.

## Key Safety Evidence
- **EFSA 2015 re-evaluation:** No safety concern at reported use levels; no numerical ADI required.
- **JECFA 1981:** ADI not specified (group ADI), consistent with low concern at food-additive exposure levels.
- **Human observational evidence (kidney stones):**
  - Prospective cohorts/meta-analytic summaries indicate risk signals mainly in **high supplemental** intakes (often around or above 1,000 mg/day), with stronger/clearer signal in men than women in several analyses.
- **High-dose clinical safety literature (non-additive, pharmacologic exposure):**
  - Scoping review of IV high-dose vitamin C reported adverse event patterns including oxalate nephropathy, hemolysis risk in G6PD deficiency, and glucometer interference; evidence base mostly low-level/case-based.
- **Mechanistic concern at high doses:** Increased oxalate production/excretion at high intakes can raise renal risk in susceptible individuals.

## Exposure Assessment
- **Typical background intake (U.S. adults):** NIH ODS reports average vitamin C intake from foods/beverages around 84.9 mg/day (men) and 67.0 mg/day (women); overall intakes with supplements are higher but generally far below pharmacologic megadose levels.
- **UL context:** NIH ODS adult upper intake level is 2,000 mg/day, far above usual intake from food additive exposure.
- **ADI exceedance risk for additive use:** Because EFSA/JECFA both effectively use “not specified / no numerical ADI,” routine dietary additive exposure does not indicate an ADI exceedance pattern.
- **Potentially vulnerable populations:**
  - People with renal impairment/history of nephrolithiasis at very high supplemental or IV exposures.
  - G6PD-deficient patients in high-dose IV vitamin C settings.
  - These concerns are tied to high-dose therapeutic/supplement contexts, not typical E300 additive use.

## Risk Assessment

### 1. Tier-by-tier analysis

#### risk_free
- **Evidence supporting this tier:**
  - Endogenous/natural nutrient; widespread dietary presence.
  - EFSA (2015) and JECFA (1981) both support non-numeric ADI/no major concern at food-additive exposure.
- **Evidence against this tier:**
  - Credible adverse-event evidence exists at high-dose supplemental/IV exposures (renal/oxalate-related and specific clinical risks), so “no credible evidence of harm” is too strong.

#### limited
- **Evidence supporting this tier:**
  - Approved/GRAS in major jurisdictions.
  - EFSA and JECFA outcomes are highly permissive (no numerical ADI / ADI not specified).
  - Safety signals mainly occur at high doses outside normal additive exposure ranges.
- **Evidence against this tier:**
  - Some epidemiologic signals (notably in men at high supplemental intakes) show risk can emerge with chronic high intake.

#### moderate
- **Evidence supporting this tier:**
  - Mechanistic plausibility and observed renal risk signals at high intakes.
- **Evidence against this tier:**
  - No EFSA/JECFA tightening to a low numeric ADI; no broad regulatory restrictions or bans for additive use; concerns are dose-context specific.

#### high
- **Evidence supporting this tier:**
  - Very limited for additive-use context.
- **Evidence against this tier:**
  - No IARC Group 2A/2B/1 classification identified for ascorbic acid.
  - No major-jurisdiction additive ban pattern.
  - EFSA/JECFA conclusions do not indicate unresolved core safety uncertainty at permitted additive exposures.

### 2. Rationale
Key drivers are: (1) strong regulatory acceptance in EU/U.S./JECFA, (2) non-numeric ADI framework from EFSA/JECFA, and (3) adverse findings concentrated at high-dose supplement/IV contexts rather than ordinary food-additive exposure.

### 3. Recommended tier
**limited**
