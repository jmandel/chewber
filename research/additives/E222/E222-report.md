# E222 Research Report: Sodium hydrogen sulfite (Sodium bisulfite)

## Identity
- **E-number:** E222 (INS 222)
- **Name:** Sodium hydrogen sulfite (common name: sodium bisulfite)
- **CAS number(s):** 7631-90-5
- **Chemical class:** Inorganic sulfite salt; sulfur dioxide-releasing preservative/antioxidant
- **Common synonyms:** Sodium bisulfite, sodium hydrogensulfite, monosodium sulfite
- **Natural vs synthetic:** **Synthetic** food additive (industrial inorganic salt)

## Function in Food
- **Primary function:** Preservative and antioxidant (WHO/JECFA functional class explicitly lists both).
- **Mechanism of action:** Sulfite/bisulfite species act as reducing agents and antimicrobial stressors; this supports anti-browning/preservation uses and microbial suppression in foods.
- **Common food categories:** Broadly food and beverages (including sulfited foods); U.S. law specifically restricts use in meats, thiamin-source foods, and raw fruits/vegetables sold or served fresh.

## Regulatory Status
### EFSA (EU)
- **2016 re-evaluation (E220-228 group):** EFSA established a **temporary group ADI of 0.7 mg/kg bw/day** (expressed as SO2 equivalents), and reported ADI exceedance in toddlers/children at mean/high intake scenarios.
- **2022 follow-up:** EFSA could not resolve toxicology uncertainties and **withdrew the temporary group ADI**.
- **2025 exposure update:** EFSA reported concern for **high consumers** based on MOE relative to a BMDL from a developmental neurotoxicity study.
- **Current interpretation:** E222 remains an authorized additive in the sulfite group, but EFSA's latest scientific position is more restrictive/uncertain than in 2016.

### FDA / eCFR (U.S.)
- **Status:** GRAS when used per GMP.
- **Citation:** **21 CFR 182.3739 (Sodium bisulfite).**
- **Use conditions in 21 CFR 182.3739(c):** Not for use in meats; in foods recognized as vitamin B1 (thiamin) sources; or on raw fruits/vegetables intended to be sold/served fresh.
- **Labeling threshold:** Sulfiting agent declaration framework includes a **10 ppm (mg/kg) detectable level** in finished food (21 CFR 101.100(a)(4)); standardized foods addressed in 21 CFR 130.9.

## Key Safety Evidence
### Animal / experimental / mechanistic
- **2022 (Toxicology Letters):** Food additive sodium bisulfite exposure altered biothiol balance, increased ROS/NF-kB signaling, and promoted pro-inflammatory responses; mouse findings supported intestinal inflammation signal.
  URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35114314/

### Microbiome-relevant evidence
- **2017 (PLOS ONE):** Sodium bisulfite/sulfite inhibited growth of tested beneficial gut bacteria at concentrations overlapping "safe" preservative ranges used in foods (experimental in vitro model).
  URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29045472/

### Allergy / sensitization context
- **2026 (Contact Dermatitis):** Sodium metabisulfite patch-test positivity was observed (2.9%) but often lacked clear clinical relevance in that study cohort.
  URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41194600/
- **2024 (Dermatitis):** Sulfites named "Allergen of the Year" for contact-allergy awareness; food/beverage and pharmaceutical exposure contexts discussed.
  URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37590472/

### Older broad safety review
- **2003 (Int J Toxicol):** Mixed genotoxicity findings and high-dose toxicity signals across sulfite salts; interpreted as low risk under typical cosmetic-use contexts in that review.
  URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14555420/

## Exposure Assessment
- EFSA 2016: reported temporary ADI exceedance in toddlers/children in some scenarios.
- EFSA 2025 update: reported potential concern for **high consumers** across several age groups based on MOE vs BMDL.
- U.S. labeling trigger at detectable sulfite levels (>=10 ppm) is intended to improve risk management for consumers needing sulfite awareness.

### Vulnerable populations
- **Children/toddlers/high consumers** (EFSA exposure concern and ADI exceedance patterns).
- **People with sulfite sensitivity/contact allergy phenotypes** (clinical allergology literature primarily concerns metabisulfite and contact contexts, but relevant to sulfite class vigilance).

## Risk Assessment
### 1. Tier-by-tier analysis

#### `risk_free`
- **Evidence supporting this tier:** Historic approvals (JECFA group ADI; FDA GRAS with GMP) and long-term food-use history.
- **Evidence against this tier:** EFSA withdrew temporary ADI (2022) and later reported MOE concern for high consumers (2025); mechanistic/inflammatory and microbiome signals exist.

#### `limited`
- **Evidence supporting this tier:** Regulatory acceptance remains in major jurisdictions; JECFA ADI established; FDA has explicit GMP framework.
- **Evidence against this tier:** EFSA's post-2016 trajectory (ADI withdrawal + high-consumer concern) goes beyond "minor concerns only at high doses" and indicates unresolved uncertainty.

#### `moderate`
- **Evidence supporting this tier:** Approved additive but with major caveats: EFSA ADI withdrawn, exposure concern in high consumers and children/toddlers, and mechanistic literature indicating plausible biological harms under some exposure conditions.
- **Evidence against this tier:** IARC does not classify sulfites/sulfur dioxide as probable/possible carcinogens (Group 3), and no broad bans were identified.

#### `high`
- **Evidence supporting this tier:** EFSA concern for high consumers and unresolved toxicology gaps could be interpreted as elevated caution.
- **Evidence against this tier:** No IARC Group 2A/2B/1 signal for sulfite class entries, no broad regulatory bans, and continued regulated use (with restrictions) in major systems.

### 2. Rationale
The strongest current regulator-weighted signals are: (a) EFSA's withdrawal of the temporary ADI due unresolved uncertainty, (b) EFSA's updated high-consumer concern, and (c) targeted but nontrivial mechanistic/allergy evidence. These are stronger than a "limited risk" profile, but do not meet "high" tier criteria (no major bans or high-level carcinogenic classification).

### 3. Recommended tier
**`moderate`**
