# E260 (Acetic acid) Research Report

## Identity
- **E-number:** E260
- **Name:** Acetic acid
- **CAS number(s):** 64-19-7
- **Chemical class:** Short-chain carboxylic acid (SCFA)
- **Common synonyms:** Ethanoic acid, vinegar acid, acetate (conjugate base)
- **Natural vs synthetic:** Both. It occurs naturally in foods and human metabolism, and is also produced industrially (fermentation and chemical synthesis).

## Function in Food
- **Primary role:** Acidity regulator and preservative.
- **Mechanism of action:** Lowers pH and increases the undissociated acid fraction; this inhibits growth of many bacteria and fungi.
- **Common food categories:** Condiments/relishes, sauces/gravies, cheeses/dairy analogs, baked goods, meat products, fats/oils, chewing gum, and other foods under GMP limits (per 21 CFR 184.1005).

## Regulatory Status

- I attempted direct search on `efsa.europa.eu` for `E260`/`acetic acid`, but the site returned anti-bot/robots restrictions in this environment.
- Fallback used: EFSA Journal record and full text (PubMed/PMC) for **"Safety evaluation of buffered vinegar as a food additive" (2022)**.
- **Approval status:** Acetic acid and salts (E260-E263) are described as authorised in the EU as Group I additives (quantum satis) in Regulation (EC) No 1333/2008 (as cited in the EFSA 2022 opinion).
- **ADI:** EFSA states the establishment of an ADI for oral intake of acetic acid is **not considered necessary** (referencing EFSA 2013). Historical SCF group ADI is **"not specified"**.
- **Latest EFSA evaluation year found for directly relevant acetic-acid additive context:** **2022** (buffered vinegar opinion, acetic acid as primary constituent).
- **Key conclusion:** No safety concern at proposed maximum/typical use levels for buffered vinegar; EFSA could not conclude for proposed Group I quantum satis use due non-estimable exposure.

### FDA / eCFR (Title 21)
- **Status:** Acetic acid is in 21 CFR Part 184 (direct food substances affirmed as GRAS).
- **Citation:** **21 CFR 184.1005 (Acetic acid)** and the general GRAS framework in **21 CFR 184.1**.
- **Conditions of use:** Current good manufacturing practice; section 184.1005 also lists typical maximum as-served levels for several food categories (for example 9.0% condiments/relishes, 3.0% sauces/gravies, 0.8% cheeses, 0.25% baked goods, 0.15% other categories).

### JECFA / WHO
- **Database entry:** Chemical 4785 (INS 260, acetic acid).
- **Current displayed evaluation year:** **2004** with statement: "No safety concern at current levels of intake when used as a flavouring agent."
- **ADI history in same record:** Group ADI **"not limited"** for acetic acid and potassium/sodium salts established at 17th meeting (1973) and maintained at 49th meeting (1997).

## Key Safety Evidence
### Animal and mechanistic/regulatory evidence
- EFSA-cited toxicology context indicates adverse effects in humans are mainly linked to **irritating/corrosive properties of concentrated acetic acid** rather than normal dietary presence.
- EFSA (2022) accepted no new Tier toxicology package for buffered vinegar because oral exposure dissociates to acetate/acetic anion, which are normal dietary/metabolic constituents.

### Epidemiological evidence
- **NutriNet-Sante cohort (BMJ, 2026)** reported positive associations between higher preservative intake and cancer incidence; for **acetic acid**, reported HR for overall cancer was 1.12 (95% CI 1.01-1.25).
- This is observational evidence and does not establish causality.

### Human high-exposure toxicity evidence
- Case-report literature includes severe outcomes after very high ingestion (e.g., multiorgan failure report; case report of lethal toxicity with massive dietary acetic acid intake plus carbamazepine).
- These reflect acute high-dose scenarios, not typical additive-level exposure.

## Exposure Assessment
- EFSA 2022 (buffered vinegar, expressed as acetic acid equivalents):
  - Mean exposure: **8.9 mg/kg bw/day (infants) to 280.3 mg/kg bw/day (children)**.
  - 95th percentile exposure: **27.9 mg/kg bw/day (infants) to 1,078 mg/kg bw/day (toddlers)**.
- EFSA-cited vinegar intake context (from prior EFSA work, as quoted in 2022 opinion):
  - Mean acetic acid intake from vinegar: **0-123 mg/person/day**.
  - 95th percentile: **0-480 mg/person/day**.
- **ADI exceedance risk:** Not directly applicable because EFSA/JECFA indicate ADI not necessary/not limited for oral intake context; exposure concern is mainly concentration-dependent irritation at high doses.
- **Potentially vulnerable groups:** Toddlers/children have highest modeled mg/kg exposure in additive scenarios; people exposed to concentrated acetic acid preparations are at acute irritation/corrosion risk.

## Risk Assessment
### 1. Tier-by-tier analysis
- **risk_free**
  - Evidence supporting this tier: Endogenous/naturally occurring metabolite; EFSA ADI not necessary; JECFA group ADI not limited; long history of authorised use.
  - Evidence against this tier: New observational epidemiology (BMJ 2026) reports association signals for acetic-acid preservative intake and cancer incidence; acute high-dose corrosive toxicity is well documented.
- **limited**
  - Evidence supporting this tier: Approved/GRAS in major jurisdictions; no serious regulatory signal at intended use levels; concerns are mainly at high concentrations or from non-causal observational findings.
  - Evidence against this tier: The BMJ cohort signal may justify closer monitoring; EFSA 2022 could not conclude for quantum satis proposal for buffered vinegar.
- **moderate**
  - Evidence supporting this tier: Presence of a recent human epidemiological signal and inability to estimate some quantum satis exposure scenarios.
  - Evidence against this tier: Core global evaluations still indicate no ADI need/not limited and no confirmed causal harm at normal additive use.
- **high**
  - Evidence supporting this tier: Concentrated acetic acid can cause severe toxicity.
  - Evidence against this tier: No IARC carcinogenic classification for acetic acid, no broad regulatory bans, and major regulators continue to permit use.

### 2. Rationale
- The weight of regulatory evidence supports low concern at normal use levels, but not a strict "no concern under all conditions" position.
- The main uncertainty driver is emerging observational epidemiology, while established toxicological concern remains concentration-dependent irritation/corrosion.

### 3. Recommended tier
- **Recommended tier: `limited`**
